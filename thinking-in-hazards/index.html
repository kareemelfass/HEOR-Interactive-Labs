How to use this tutorial

Start with the examples, then read the equations. The first five parts explain what the hazard is, how it is estimated and what an HR can mean. The remaining parts explain how to use that information in health economics and outcomes research (HEOR). Mathematics is introduced only when it helps explain a calculation. All numerical examples are illustrative unless explicitly attributed to a study. Expected counts may be fractional because they describe an average across hypothetical repetitions, not actual patients. Time units are stated in each example. A survival function always refers to a specific event definition and time origin.

Three questions to keep separate: What does a statistical model estimate? What does the estimate mean for patients or populations? Can it legitimately be used as an economic model input? A mathematically correct transformation can still be clinically inappropriate.

Part I. Understanding hazards from first principles

1. Why do we need the concept of hazard?

The problem. Imagine two cancer trials, each with 100 patients. In both, 20 patients die during the first year. In trial A all 20 deaths occur at the end of month 1; in trial B they occur at the end of month 11. If we report only 20% mortality, the trials look identical. But patients in B have more time alive, which matters clinically and economically.

Suppose no further deaths occur that year. Trial A contributes 80 × 12 + 20 × 1 = 980 person-months alive. Trial B contributes 80 × 12 + 20 × 11 = 1,180 person-months. The same event count conceals a difference of 200 person-months. A person-month means one person observed or alive for one month, depending on the context. The average survival time within the year is 9.8 versus 11.8 months. This is a difference we can translate into life years.

Now suppose several patients leave the study before month 12. We cannot conclude that they lived to month 12, but we know they lived at least until their last observation. Such incomplete follow-up is called right censoring. A patient censored at month 5 supplies five months of observed event-free time; their later event time is unknown.

A risk set is the group still eligible to experience the event, and under observation, immediately before a particular time. After a patient dies, they cannot die again. After a patient is censored, we no longer observe their subsequent experience. These groups change throughout follow-up.

Biostatistics: binary regression can answer a fixed-horizon risk question when outcome status is known at that horizon, but simply ignoring time and censoring will generally answer a different question. Survival analysis allows us to use partial information about event times.

HEOR: two interventions with equal five-year mortality may still generate different survival-related costs and benefits because the timing of death differs.

Remember: survival analysis asks both whether and when an event occurs. The hazard describes the changing rate at which events occur. [1, pp. 3–8]

2. What exactly is a hazard?

Imagine an uncensored cohort of 100 patients. During month 1, 10 die. During month 2, nine more die. In month 3, another nine die.

Month Alive at start Deaths Alive at end Conditional monthly death probability
1 100 10 90 10.00%
2 90 9 81 10.00%
3 81 9 72 11.11%

Nine deaths in months 2 and 3 do not mean equal risk. The denominator became smaller. Notice, however, that 9/81 is an interval probability, not an instantaneous hazard. We need the time spent alive to estimate an event rate. If all nine deaths in month 2 happened halfway through, total exposure in that month would be 81 × 1 + 9 × 0.5 = 85.5 person-months. The observed rate would be 9/85.5 = 0.1053 deaths per person-month.

Rate versus probability

A speed of 60 km/hour does not say how far you travelled without knowing the travel time. Similarly, a hazard of 0.2/year does not say what fraction of people will die unless we know the period and how the hazard behaves throughout it. The analogy is about units, not an assertion that each individual literally travels toward death at a fixed speed.

If the hazard stays constant at 0.2/year, the exact annual death probability is 1−e−0.2=18.13%1-e^{-0.2}=18.13\%. Over a month it is 1−e−0.2/12=1.65%1-e^{-0.2/12}=1.65\%. A hazard of 2/year is entirely possible: the corresponding annual probability is 86.47%, not 200%.

A hazard describes the event rate among individuals still event-free at that moment. For a short period of length Δt\Delta t, the chance of an event is approximately the hazard multiplied by the period length. Shrink that period towards zero and the approximation becomes the definition:

h(t)=limΔt→0P(t≤T<t+Δt∣T≥t)Δt.h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t}.

Read the numerator as: among those who have survived to time tt, how likely is the event in the next tiny interval? Dividing by its length expresses the probability as a rate. At a precise instant a continuous event time has probability zero, but its rate need not be zero.

Concept Units Example

Instantaneous hazard Inverse time 0.105/year at year 3
Observed incidence rate Events/person-time 9/85.5 person-months

Why hazards change

A post-operative complication hazard may decline as patients recover. A chronic disease mortality hazard may rise as disease worsens. A treatment toxicity hazard may peak early, and ageing may increase mortality later. These descriptions concern rates, not the shape of cumulative risk, which never falls for a first terminal event. Population hazard can also decline because high-risk individuals leave the risk set earlier, even if no individual’s own rate declines (Chapter 12).

Remember: the hazard is a rate, not a probability; the risk set and time unit matter. [1, pp. 10–12, 33]

3. How hazards turn into survival

Consider an annual mortality hazard of 0.2, constant for three years. Each year adds 0.2 to the cumulative hazard. The chance of being alive at year 3 reflects all three years, not just the third year.

Year Cumulative hazard Survival Cumulative death probability
0 0.0000 100.00% 0.00%
1 0.2000 81.87% 18.13%
2 0.4000 67.03% 32.97%
3 0.6000 54.88% 45.12%

The accumulated hazard is called the cumulative hazard, H(t)H(t). It has no time unit: a rate per year multiplied by years is dimensionless. It can exceed 1. Survival is the exponential of the negative cumulative hazard:

H(t)=∫0th(u)du,S(t)=e−H(t).H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du,\qquad S(t)=e^{-H(t)}.

An integral is simply the total obtained by adding many tiny contributions of hazard × time. No advanced calculus is needed to understand a piecewise-constant example: in year 1 a hazard of 0.1 contributes 0.1; in year 2 a hazard of 0.3 contributes 0.3. At year 2, H=0.4H=0.4 and S=e−0.4=0.6703S=e^{-0.4}=0.6703.

Why the exponential? Split a period into very small pieces. The probability of surviving each piece is approximately 1−hΔt1-h\Delta t. The probability of surviving the whole period is the product of those conditional probabilities. In the limit, the product becomes e−H(t)e^{-H(t)}. This is why the relationship is nonlinear.

The inverse operation is a logarithm: H(t)=−logS(t)H(t)=-\log S(t). A derivative is the local slope of a function. The instantaneous hazard can be recovered from the local slope of cumulative hazard, or the negative slope of log survival.

Function Meaning Relationship

Cumulative distribution F(t)F(t) Probability event occurred by time t 1−S(t)1-S(t)
Density f(t)f(t) Event probability per tiny time interval in original cohort h(t)S(t)h(t)S(t)

A density is not a probability at a point. For example, at year 3 with constant hazard 0.2, f(3)=0.2e−0.6=0.10976f(3)=0.2e^{-0.6}=0.10976/year. Roughly 1.10% of the original cohort dies in the next 0.1 year, while roughly 2% of those still alive dies in that interval. The exact numbers differ slightly because the interval is finite.

HEOR: survival area is expected time alive. Integrating a survival curve, rather than integrating its hazard, produces life years. If a patient can be genuinely cured of a non-death endpoint, survival need not tend to zero; all-cause survival ultimately should.

Remember: hazard is current rate; cumulative hazard is history; survival is the fraction expected to remain event-free; density accounts for the fraction still at risk. [1, pp. 9–14]

4. Conditional survival: isolating one interval

Suppose survival is 80% at year 1 and 60% at year 2. In an expected cohort of 100, 80 survive to year 1 and 60 to year 2. During year 2, 20 of the 80 die. The conditional probability of death is 20/80 = 25%, not 20%. The latter is the probability, measured from baseline, of dying specifically during year 2.

qi=S(ti)S(ti−1),pi=1−qi,ΔHi=−logqi.q_i=\frac{S(t_i)}{S(t_{i-1})},\quad p_i=1-q_i,\quad \Delta H_i=-\log q_i.

Here qiq_i is conditional survival and pip_i is conditional event probability. With q=0.75q=0.75, ΔH=0.28768\Delta H=0.28768. If the period is one year, its time-average hazard is 0.28768/year. This does not prove that the hazard was constant during that year.

To rebuild a curve, multiply successive conditional survivals. If year 1 survival is 0.8 and year 2 conditional survival is 0.75, cumulative survival is 0.8×0.75=0.60.8\times0.75=0.6.

Changing cycle length

An annual single-exit event probability of 30% corresponds to a cumulative hazard of −log(0.7)=0.356675-\log(0.7)=0.356675. If the hazard is constant throughout that year, monthly probability is 1−0.71/12=2.93%1-0.7^{1/12}=2.93\%. Dividing 30% by 12 gives 2.5%, which is not equivalent: twelve months compounded at 2.5% imply an annual risk of about 26.2%.

An exponential event time has constant hazard and is memoryless: conditional remaining survival depends only on the length of the next interval. A Weibull hazard that increases with time does not have this property. In a semi-Markov model, duration in a state may therefore matter.

Remember: dividing survival estimates removes prior history. Multiplying conditional survival puts history back together. [3, §§3.3–3.5]

Kaplan–Meier (KM) multiplies conditional survival at each observed death time. Nelson–Aalen (NA) accumulates d/nd/n, where dd is the number of deaths and nn the risk set just before death. With one death among ten, KM multiplies by 0.9; NA adds 0.1.

Negative log KM is close to NA when each event fraction is small, but not identical: −log(1−d/n)-\log(1-d/n) exceeds d/nd/n. Both are step estimates. To obtain an instantaneous hazard curve we need smoothing or a model; a step in a KM curve is not itself a smooth instantaneous hazard. Kernel smoothing averages nearby event rates, with the bandwidth controlling how smooth the estimate is. Wider smoothing can hide genuine peaks; very narrow smoothing can create noisy peaks.

A life table groups time into intervals and accounts approximately for withdrawals when exact event times are unavailable. Dividing interval events by observed person-time estimates an observed interval rate. Neither a life table nor a smoothed curve resolves informative censoring by itself.

Why the likelihood uses both hazard and survival

For a patient who dies at month 2, the model must account for surviving to month 2 and then dying around that time. The density is h(2)S(2)h(2)S(2). For a patient censored at month 2, we only know they survived to that time, so the contribution is S(2)S(2). Let δ\delta equal 1 for event and 0 for censoring. The contribution is h(t)δS(t)h(t)^\delta S(t), ignoring the censoring distribution under the relevant independence conditions.

For a constant death hazard of 0.2/month, a death at month 2 contributes 0.2e−0.4=0.13410.2e^{-0.4}=0.1341 (a density), and censoring at month 2 contributes e−0.4=0.6703e^{-0.4}=0.6703 (a probability). Multiplying contributions across patients and choosing the parameter with largest likelihood fits the model. With a constant hazard the maximum likelihood estimate is total deaths divided by total observed person-time: 5/40 = 0.1250/month for this dataset.

Other observation mechanisms

Left truncation (delayed entry) means individuals join the risk set only after surviving to their entry time. Interval censoring means an event is known to occur between two visits, not exactly when. These require corresponding risk-set or likelihood changes. If sicker people drop out, ordinary KM may overestimate survival; inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) can help if the censoring model and underlying assumptions are adequate.

6. Where does the baseline hazard come from?

The word baseline is misleading. A baseline hazard is not the hazard on day zero. It is an entire hazard function for a reference covariate profile. In Cox regression, that profile is all model predictors equal to zero, unless we re-express the baseline relative to another profile.

Imagine a model in which untreated low-risk patients have a hazard of 0.1/year in year 1 and 0.2/year in year 2. High-risk status multiplies the hazard by 3, and treatment multiplies it by 0.6. The model has no treatment-by-risk interaction.

Profile Multiplier Year 1 hazard Year 2 hazard Year 2 survival
Untreated low risk 1.0000 0.1000 0.2000 74.08%
Treated low risk 0.6000 0.0600 0.1200 83.53%
Untreated high risk 3.0000 0.3000 0.6000 40.66%
Treated high risk 1.8000 0.1800 0.3600 58.27%

The baseline function captures time. The regression coefficients capture how predictors change its level. In notation, h(t∣x)=h0(t)exp(xTβ)h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp(x^T\beta). For fixed predictors the same multiplier applies to cumulative hazard, so S(t∣x)=exp[−H0(t)exp(xTβ)]S(t\mid x)=\exp[-H_0(t)\exp(x^T\beta)].

Estimating rather than assuming the baseline

Cox regression estimates the coefficients by comparing patients at event times. Once those coefficients are available, a Breslow baseline cumulative hazard estimate adds dj/∑i∈Rjexp(xiTβ̂)d_j/\sum_{i\in R_j}\exp(x_i^T\hat\beta) at each event time. If one death occurs in a risk set of four patients with multipliers 1, 1, 2 and 2, the estimated baseline increment is 1/6. A patient with multiplier 2 accrues 2/6 on the cumulative hazard scale. This illustrates the calculation, not an assertion that the multipliers were estimated from that one event. Tied-event methods may modify this calculation.

An untreated control arm contains people with different ages and risk factors. Its marginal survival is not necessarily the Cox baseline survival. If age is coded as raw years, age zero may be a reference outside the population. Centre age at 60 to give a more meaningful reference. This changes the numerical baseline, not the predictions for a given patient.

A parametric model estimates a baseline shape and parameters from data. For example, a Weibull model estimates a shape and scale. A flexible model estimates spline coefficients. A trial HR alone does not identify any of these baseline functions.

HEOR: before using a baseline curve, identify whose survival it represents. Is it a reference patient, the average of predictions over patients, the control-arm KM curve or a life table? These are not interchangeable. [1, pp. 16–19; 2, terminology box]

7. The hazard ratio without ambiguity

At a particular time, a control hazard of 0.4/year and a treatment hazard of 0.28/year give HR = 0.28/0.4 = 0.70. The numerator and denominator have the same time unit, so the ratio has no units. In the two groups still at risk at that time, the treatment group has a 30% lower event rate. That does not tell us the percentage who die during a year, nor how long each patient’s life is extended.

Under a constant HR throughout follow-up, the same relative hazard reduction produces different absolute benefits depending on the reference survival. The calculation is p1=1−(1−p0)HRp_1=1-(1-p_0)^{HR}, derived in Chapter 18.

Control risk Treatment risk Risk ratio Absolute risk reduction (pp)
5.00% 3.53% 0.7054 1.47
20.00% 14.46% 0.7231 5.54

The risk ratio divides the cumulative probabilities; the odds ratio divides p/(1−p)p/(1-p); the incidence rate ratio divides events/person-time; the time ratio compares event-time quantiles under an AFT model. None is generally equal to an HR. All depend on the endpoint definition and the reference group.

Confidence intervals and reporting

Most regression models estimate a coefficient on the log hazard scale, so HR=eβHR=e^\beta. If HR = 0.70 and the standard error of log HR is 0.15, then β=log(0.7)=−0.3567\beta=\log(0.7)=-0.3567. An approximate 95% confidence interval is exp(β±1.96×0.15)\exp(\beta\pm1.96\times0.15), or 0.52 to 0.94. Test the null β=0\beta=0, equivalent to HR = 1. Report the model, endpoint, follow-up, direction and interval. A statistically significant HR may have little clinical importance when few events occur. Conversely a wide interval including 1 is not evidence that treatments are equivalent.

Better reporting: “The estimated death hazard ratio for A versus B was 0.70 (95% CI 0.52–0.94), under the proportional hazards model.” Accompany it with survival probabilities and an absolute survival contrast. When PH is inappropriate, explicitly identify the single HR as a model-based summary rather than a constant reduction.

8. Models that estimate or describe hazards

Cox regression: comparing patients at events

At one event time suppose four patients are still at risk, two treated and two untreated. If treatment has HR rr, the total relative hazard weight is 2+2r2+2r. The model-based probability that a particular treated patient is the one who dies, conditional on one death at that time, is r/(2+2r)r/(2+2r). If r=0.5r=0.5, this is 1/6. For a particular untreated patient it is 1/3. Notice that the common baseline hazard cancels. Repeating these comparisons across observed event times yields the partial likelihood, which is the part of the likelihood used to estimate coefficients without choosing a parametric baseline shape. After fitting, estimate the cumulative baseline separately (Chapter 6).

Parametric models: choosing a shape

An exponential model describes a constant hazard h(t)=λh(t)=\lambda. A Weibull PH parameterisation uses H(t)=λtkH(t)=\lambda t^k, h(t)=λktk−1h(t)=\lambda kt^{k-1}. Here k is the shape, and λ\lambda has units depending on k. If k=1 it becomes exponential; k>1 yields increasing hazard; 0<k<1 yields decreasing hazard. For k=2 and λ=0.1\lambda=0.1, the rate at year 1 is 0.2/year and at year 2 is 0.4/year. A Gompertz model uses h(t)=aebth(t)=a e^{bt} and can increase or decrease with time. A negative Gompertz shape without background mortality can imply a positive long-term survival plateau, which is unsuitable for unconstrained all-cause mortality in many settings.

An accelerated failure time (AFT) model acts on time rather than the hazard. If T1=1.5T0T_1=1.5T_0 in distribution, the treated median is 1.5 times the control median. With the conventional model logT=α+xβ+σϵ\log T=\alpha+x\beta+\sigma\epsilon, the time ratio is eβe^\beta. Weibull supports both AFT and PH formulations: for shape k, HR = time ratio raised to −k. The exponential is its k=1 special case. Other families, such as log-normal and log-logistic, generally do not give time-constant HRs when treatment shifts the AFT location.

Additive and flexible models

Aalen’s additive hazard model changes rates by adding coefficients rather than multiplying. A reduction of 0.02/year is a hazard difference, not a probability difference. With baseline hazard 0.1/year it gives 0.08/year; with baseline 0.5/year it gives 0.48/year. Ensure resulting predicted rates are meaningful.

A spline is a flexible function assembled from smooth pieces joined around knots. A model may represent the hazard directly using nonnegative M-spline basis functions. If h0(t)=∑jajMj(t)h_0(t)=\sum_j a_j M_j(t) with nonnegative weights, integration gives H0(t)=∑jajIj(t)H_0(t)=\sum_j a_j I_j(t), where I-splines are integrated M-splines (with the appropriate origin). A Royston–Parmar model instead often uses restricted cubic splines on log cumulative hazard against log time. These are different models, not interchangeable names. Each requires checking the resulting hazard shape and long-term extrapolation.

Worked comparison: For a reference median of 3 years, fit or set a Weibull shape of 2. The rate parameter is log(2)/9\log(2)/9. A treatment time ratio of 1.5 yields HR 1.5−2=0.44441.5^{-2}=0.4444. The treated median is 4.5 years. If instead the exponential shape were 1, the same time ratio would imply HR 0.6667. This demonstrates why HR and time ratio are different quantities.

Remember: choose a statistical model for the scientific question, then check the hazard and survival predictions. [1, pp. 18–24; 10]

Part III. Proportional and non-proportional hazards

9. What does proportional hazards really mean?

Imagine the reference annual hazard is 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4 in three successive years. Treatment hazards are 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2. The disease is becoming more dangerous in both groups, but treatment halves the rate at every moment. Both hazards change; their ratio does not. That is proportional hazards (PH).

Year Control hazard Treatment hazard HR Control survival Treatment survival
1 0.10 0.05 0.50 90.48% 95.12%
2 0.20 0.10 0.50 74.08% 86.07%
3 0.40 0.20 0.50 49.66% 70.47%

The survival curves do not have to be parallel. They are both decreasing and separated by a changing absolute distance. In this example, the log cumulative hazards differ by log(0.5)\log(0.5) at every positive time. A plot of log cumulative hazard against time or log time can therefore help assess PH. Survival curves may appear to cross by sampling variation even when the underlying curves do not. Conversely, non-crossing survival curves do not establish PH.

How to investigate PH

First, examine the clinical mechanism. A surgical procedure may create early mortality followed by later benefit; an immune therapy may need time to take effect. Second, inspect the KM curves and smoothed hazard estimates (with caution in sparse tails). Third, check covariate-specific and global tests based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals. A residual is the difference between a covariate value observed at an event and the value expected from its risk set under the model. A pattern over time suggests the model’s coefficient may need to change with time. The test is not a yes-or-no verdict: small studies may lack power, large studies may detect small practically unimportant departures.

Finally, ask whether the departure matters for the actual estimand or prediction. A slight deviation over the last few weeks of follow-up may matter little to a fixed 6-month risk estimate but might matter to lifetime extrapolation if perpetuated. Diagnostic plots, effect-by-time estimates, clinical knowledge and alternative model predictions are complementary.

Remember: PH means the ratio is constant, not that hazards or absolute survival differences are constant. [1, pp. 17, 21–22; 2]

10. When the hazard ratio changes with time

A hypothetical immunotherapy has early toxicity and delayed clinical benefit. Chemotherapy has a constant death hazard of 0.07/month. For the first six months, immunotherapy has HR 1.15, so its hazard is 0.0805/month. After month 6, HR is 0.60, so its hazard falls to 0.042/month. The reference hazard has not changed. If both treatments had followed the same hazard, they would have equal survival; here early harm leaves fewer patients alive at month 6. The later lower hazard must compensate for that accumulated disadvantage before survival curves cross.

What does Cox estimate if PH is false?

The partial likelihood looks for one coefficient that best explains observed failures across risk sets. When the true coefficient changes, the estimate is a compromise determined by the events and the composition of those risk sets. Early deaths, administrative end times and censoring can change the compromise. It is not generally a simple arithmetic mean of the early and late HRs, nor necessarily a transportable treatment effect. A single HR may still be a prespecified statistical summary, but interpret its estimand and limitations explicitly. [4; 7, 8]

Choosing a response

If the scientific question is about the timing of benefit, estimate a piecewise HR or a smoothly time-varying treatment coefficient. Choose breakpoints prospectively or clinically where possible. If another covariate violates PH, a stratified Cox model gives that covariate separate baseline hazards; if treatment itself is used as the stratum, there is no single estimated treatment coefficient. Fit separate parametric arm models when absolute survival is the main target, checking the resulting HR and tail. If a test of survival difference is primary, weighted log-rank or prespecified combination tests can help detect departures from PH, but a test statistic does not supply a treatment effect size. RMST or landmark risks are often clearer summaries (Part V).

Treatment waning is one potential cause of non-PH, not a synonym for it. Changing survivor composition alone can produce a varying marginal HR, even when treatment works identically within every risk stratum.

HEOR: applying an averaged HR to every future cycle imposes a constant effect that the underlying trial may not support. [3 §7.6; 7, 12]

11. Does a PH violation invalidate prediction?

A model may estimate the wrong instantaneous rate yet predict the right accumulated event probability at a selected time. Here is a simple mathematical example, not a reproduction of a fitted Cox model. A true annual hazard is 0.4 in year 1 and 0.1 in year 2. A simplified model uses 0.25 in both years. Their cumulative hazards are equal at year 2 (0.5). Both predict year-2 survival of 0.6065. At year 1, however, true survival is 0.6703 versus 0.7788 in the simplified model. The models can match at one prediction horizon while making different predictions earlier or later.

Calibration means agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, typically across people or groups at a specified time horizon. Discrimination means the ability to distinguish individuals who experience events earlier from those who do not. Neither is a test of the causal interpretation of a coefficient. Neither alone justifies extrapolation.

Published study: Austin and Giardiello (2025)

The authors investigated how PH violations affected calibration in Cox clinical prediction models [5]. Their data-generating models were informed by a cohort of 19,559 patients hospitalised with acute myocardial infarction in Ontario in 2016. They simulated samples of 2,000, allowing a coefficient for a continuous or binary covariate to change linearly with time on the log hazard scale. Nine magnitudes of violation for each type gave 18 scenarios, with 1,000 datasets per scenario. Comparators included AFT, flexible spline models and regression on pseudo-observations (constructed outcomes used for predicting absolute risks at a given time); stratified Cox models were also considered for binary covariates.

They reported negligible impact on calibration in the settings investigated, despite violations of PH. This result concerns prediction performance in their simulation design, not the validity of a constant HR as a treatment effect. It should not be generalised to arbitrary delayed treatment effects, cure fractions, strong crossing hazards or the unobserved decades of an economic model. The study demonstrates why a diagnostic failure need not mean every model output is equally wrong.

Worked validation plan: fit an ordinary Cox model and a model with a time interaction to the same data. Compare observed and predicted risks at clinically meaningful horizons, potentially using calibration curves, and inspect subgroup calibration. Use external data for extrapolation, not the fact that calibration was satisfactory at one internal horizon. A good probability estimate cannot identify the correct underlying hazard trajectory by itself.

This phenomenon is depletion of susceptible individuals. Frailty is unmeasured susceptibility to an event. A frailty model introduces an unobserved random effect that changes each subject’s hazard. The two-group example represents frailty in a simple discrete way. Even a marginal HR above 1 at some time can occur in more extreme mixtures despite better survival under treatment in each stratum; the marginal hazard and cumulative survival need not tell the same story.

Why randomisation does not automatically solve this

Randomisation makes treatment independent of baseline prognostic factors before follow-up, in expectation. Survival is affected by both treatment and prognosis. Conditioning on survival selects different people in each arm. A variable affected by two other variables is called a collider; conditioning on it can create an association between its causes. Think of selecting people who survived: in the control group, high-risk status makes survival harder; in the treatment group, effective treatment partly protects those patients. The selected groups are no longer comparable in risk composition.

Hernán [7] stresses the difficulty of interpreting HRs as causal effects in such settings. Bartlett et al. [2] distinguish two claims: the population hazard under an assigned treatment strategy is a meaningful counterfactual population quantity, so its ratio can be causal under identification assumptions; but it compares the survivors under each strategy, not the same fixed set of individuals. Both perspectives warn against interpreting an HR as the uniform effect treatment would have on each patient.

Remember: a change in the HR may reflect how the population changes, not a change in the biological treatment effect. This matters directly when assuming that observed late HRs represent treatment waning.

13. Conditional versus marginal: the patient is not the population

The last example makes it possible to understand three words often presented as abstract statistical jargon.

Conditional on covariates means restricting the comparison to a particular profile, such as high-risk patients. Marginal over covariates means comparing populations that include all profiles. Both instantaneous hazards still condition on remaining event-free; marginal does not mean ignoring survival.

Suppose 50% of an initial population is low-risk and 50% high-risk. The marginal survival at t is the average of the two subgroup probabilities, with the original weights 0.5 and 0.5. But the marginal hazard at t is calculated among those still alive. At t=5 the remaining subgroup fractions are no longer 50/50. Thus:

Sz(t)=∑kwkSzk(t),hz(t)=∑kwkSzk(t)hzk(t)∑kwkSzk(t).S_z(t)=\sum_k w_k S_{zk}(t),\qquad h_z(t)=\frac{\sum_k w_k S_{zk}(t)h_{zk}(t)}{\sum_k w_k S_{zk}(t)}.

The second formula just says: expected instantaneous events divided by the expected number still alive. It follows from h=f/Sh=f/S. It is not an arithmetic average of the original subgroup rates except at baseline or in special cases.

Non-collapsibility (why averaging changes an HR)

An effect measure is non-collapsible when the marginal contrast can differ from the common conditional contrast even without confounding. Chapter 12 demonstrates this exactly. The treatment HR is 0.5 in each stratum. The marginal HR can differ even though the original groups contain identical proportions of high- and low-risk patients. Confounding is a different phenomenon: treatment allocation is associated with causes of the outcome at baseline. Effect modification means treatment’s effect differs across strata on the chosen scale. We have neither in the hypothetical example, yet the marginal HR changes.

How adjustment methods work

Direct standardisation predicts each target patient’s survival under CRRT and IHD, then averages the two sets of predictions. A propensity score is the probability of receiving CRRT given baseline characteristics. Here high-risk patients have probability 0.8 and low-risk patients 0.2 in the pooled sample. In inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), the treated receive weights 1/e and the untreated 1/(1−e). The weighted CRRT high-risk count is 80/0.8=100; the low-risk count is 20/0.2=100. The weighted IHD counts similarly become 100 and 100. Under adequate assumptions, these standardise to the whole eligible study population, not an average patient.

Matching balances selected comparable patients; its target depends on the matching design. Overlap weighting assigns treated weights 1−e and untreated weights e, targeting profiles for which either treatment is relatively plausible. Augmented IPTW (AIPTW) combines outcome prediction and treatment weighting; its robustness to misspecification depends on the estimator and censoring conditions. Inverse probability of censoring weights may additionally be needed when dropout depends on measured prognostic variables. Proper uncertainty estimation must account for the fitted weights and matching or clustering.

Before estimating an effect

Define the target population, time zero, strategies, endpoint, intercurrent events (events after treatment start that affect interpretation, such as switching), and the outcome contrast. Identification requires consistency (observed outcome agrees with the intervention actually received), sufficient control of confounding and positivity (both strategies are possible in the relevant profiles), plus suitable censoring and measurement assumptions. Time-dependent confounding may need specialised longitudinal methods rather than baseline IPTW. Even perfectly adjusted marginal hazards still compare the different survivor populations.

15. What is the average hazard?

Three patients are followed without loss to follow-up for up to four years. One dies at year 1, another at year 2, and the last lives beyond year 4. There are two deaths and 1+2+4=7 person-years alive. The rate is 2/7=0.2857 deaths per person-year over this four-year window. In a larger cohort with censoring, we would like a quantity that describes the same complete-follow-up rate even when some patient information is missing.

At the four-year horizon, cumulative death probability is 2/3. Average time alive up to that horizon is 7/3 years. This is the restricted mean survival time (RMST), the expected time alive or event-free before a fixed time τ\tau. Divide these two population quantities:

AH(τ)=1−S(τ)RMST(τ),RMST(τ)=∫0τS(t)dt.AH(\tau)=\frac{1-S(\tau)}{RMST(\tau)},\qquad RMST(\tau)=\int_0^\tau S(t)dt.

If patients want to know their chance of being alive at one year, report a survival probability or risk difference. If a researcher wants an absolute person-time event rate under each intervention over a specified period, AH provides that summary. If economic modelling needs life years, costs and transitions, the full curves or appropriate transition-specific hazards are required.

20. Combining, separating and transforming hazards

Suppose a model has a baseline death hazard of 0.2/year, a treatment HR of 0.7 and an age effect HR of 1.5 for a 10-year increment. If both coefficients belong to the same log-additive model with no interaction, the predicted hazard for a treated patient ten years older is 0.2×0.7×1.5=0.210.2\times0.7\times1.5=0.21/year. This multiplication is a property of that model. It does not justify multiplying HRs from unrelated studies, adjusting twice for the same risk factor, or assuming away interactions.

Additive hazards and excess mortality

If disease-specific excess mortality is 0.15/year and background mortality is 0.05/year, the total hazard is 0.20/year. A treatment halving only excess mortality gives 0.05+0.5(0.15)=0.1250.05+0.5(0.15)=0.125/year. Halving total mortality instead gives 0.10/year, also reducing the background contribution without justification. Under constant rates, five-year survival is 0.5353 versus 0.6065, respectively. The difference is important for economic modelling.

Distinct cause-specific hazards add because, within a very small interval among those alive, the possible first causes are mutually exclusive. Statistical independence between hypothetical latent event times is not required for this observed cause-specific identity. An externally sourced background hazard plus excess hazard, however, requires a relative-survival modelling assumption about the relevance of the general population reference.

Reversing and chaining HRs

If B versus A HR is 0.8, A versus B HR is 1/0.8=1.25, under the same comparison. If C versus A HR is 0.6, C versus B HR is 0.6/0.8=0.75. This works for compatible treatment contrasts at a common time or under PH. It assumes a meaningful common population and comparison structure. An indirect estimate’s uncertainty depends on the variance and covariance of log effects. It should not be derived from point estimates alone when inference is needed.

Pitfall: subtracting background mortality from a noisy all-cause extrapolation can produce negative excess rates. Investigate population differences, sampling variation and modelling choices; clipping at zero is a constraint, not a scientific explanation.

21. Competing risks: when one hazard changes another event’s probability

Imagine a progression-free patient can progress or die before progressing. These are competing first exits. If a person dies first, they cannot subsequently have a first progression observed as a living patient. The instantaneous progression hazard is calculated among those alive and progression-free. Death changes how many patients remain eligible to progress, even if it has no direct impact on the progression rate itself.

Take a constant progression hazard of 0.3/year. Compare death hazards of 0.1/year (A) and 0.5/year (B). The one-year probability of any first exit is 1−e−(hP+hD)1-e^{-(h_P+h_D)}. With constant rates, the progression share of first exits is hP/(hP+hD)h_P/(h_P+h_D), so progression probability is that share times the any-exit probability.

The progression hazard is identical. Yet fewer patients progress in B because more die before progression. This is why a cause-specific HR cannot tell us the cause-specific cumulative probability on its own. In general, the cumulative incidence function (CIF) is Fk(t)=∫0tSallexits(u)hk(u)duF_k(t)=\int_0^t S_{all\ exits}(u)h_k(u)du. Here survival means free of every first exit, not merely alive. Estimate the CIF with the Aalen–Johansen estimator, which combines cause-specific events and the probability of staying at risk.

Cause-specific Cox models can remove people from the risk set at another event’s occurrence (often coded as censoring for model fitting). That is legitimate for estimating a cause-specific hazard. It does not make the complement of a cause-specific KM estimate the actual incidence of the event in the presence of competing deaths. The latter behaves like a net-risk calculation rather than the observed crude probability.

Fine–Gray models instead use a subdistribution hazard, defined with a modified risk set that conceptually retains individuals experiencing competing events, with appropriate weighting for censoring. The model describes changes in the CIF on a transformed scale. Its HR is not an instantaneous rate of transition out of the actual living health state. A Fine–Gray HR can inform CIF prediction with a compatible baseline subdistribution; it cannot simply replace a cause-specific transition HR in a multi-state model.

Remember: to predict the probability of a specific exit, account for all competing exits. [1 pp. 25–27; 15]

25. Semi-Markov and multi-state hazards

The same hazard may be a function of different clocks. Suppose post-progression mortality is high immediately after progression (0.6/year during the first two years in PD), then falls to 0.2/year. Patient A progresses in year 1, patient B in year 4. At model year 5, A has spent four years in PD and has hazard 0.2/year; B has spent one year in PD and has hazard 0.6/year. A model that uses only time since baseline would assign them the same post-progression hazard unless it tracks their history.

A clock-forward model uses time since the common origin, such as trial entry. A clock-reset model uses time since state entry, such as progression. A semi-Markov model allows transition rates to depend on time spent in the current state; additional dependence on age or previous history may also be needed. A tunnel-state model splits PD into duration bands (for example PD year 1, PD year 2, PD year 3+) to approximate such dependence in a cohort model. A patient-level model can track exact durations when needed, but is not the only solution.

26. Hazards in partitioned survival models

In a conventional oncology partitioned survival model (PSM), PFS is progression-free survival, OS is overall survival, and the proportion alive with progressed disease is OS − PFS. PFS is survival free of progression or death (the precise trial endpoint definition must be checked). OS includes deaths both before and after progression. Time to progression (TTP) may treat death differently, commonly as a competing event or censoring according to the reported estimand. Time on treatment (TOT) describes the duration of treatment exposure or discontinuation under a specified definition.

For patients in PF, the composite PFS hazard is the sum of progression and PF→death hazards. The OS hazard at any time is a mixture of death hazards in PF and PD, weighted by proportions of survivors in those states. It is not a single transition hazard. Thus independent PFS and OS curves determine occupancy but generally do not identify the three underlying transition intensities. Different pathways can yield the same PFS and OS curves.

27. Life tables, SMRs and excess mortality

A national life table may supply age-specific annual probabilities of death, q(x), or central mortality rates, m(x). These are different quantities. Treatment discontinuation means patients stop receiving the drug. Treatment waning means its effect changes. They may coincide, but need not. 31. Population adjustment and the average-patient approximation

Suppose trial A has IPD comparing B to A, while another trial reports aggregate data comparing C to A. Patient profiles differ across trials. Prognostic factors predict outcomes; effect modifiers change the comparative treatment effect on the chosen scale. Because HRs are non-collapsible, prognostic factors can influence marginal HRs even without conditional effect modification. Before making an indirect comparison, decide which estimand and target population are needed.

Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) reweights IPD to match reported aggregate characteristics of a target trial population. Simulated treatment comparison (STC) uses an outcome regression with covariates; appropriately marginalised STC predicts outcomes across the target population, while simply substituting target means can give a conditional estimand. Multilevel network meta-regression (ML-NMR) combines individual and aggregate trial evidence through a common individual-level model, integrating over aggregate-study covariate distributions to connect individual predictions with aggregate outcomes. Each requires assumptions about the relevant covariates, common support and treatment contrasts. Unanchored comparisons also require adequate control of all important prognostic factors and effect modifiers, a stronger assumption than a valid anchored comparison [11].

33. Recurrent events and joint event processes

A heart failure patient’s first hospitalisation is not the final event of interest. If costs are driven by all hospitalisations, a time-to-first-event HR ignores repeats. Suppose over one year 20 of 100 patients are hospitalised at least once, and those 20 have three admissions each. First-event risk is 20%, but total admissions are 60. The first-event analysis cannot tell whether the same people had one or three admissions.

A counting process records the number of observed events by each time. Andersen–Gill models use repeated start-stop intervals and model a recurrent-event intensity, often with clustering-robust standard errors. Prentice–Williams–Peterson models stratify risk by event order and use total time or gap time since the prior event; patients enter the risk set for event 2 only after event 1. Frailty models introduce shared patient susceptibility and can capture within-person dependence through a model. Model choice depends on whether the target is event intensity, time to successive events, expected count or the joint behaviour of recurrences and death. Robust standard errors adjust inference for dependence; they do not rectify an incorrectly specified mean intensity.

If a treatment has an expected 0.6 admissions per patient-year and comparator 0.9 in a common target window with comparable time denominators, the difference is 0.3 admissions per patient-year. At $5,000 per admission, that is $1,500 per patient-year of corresponding utilisation savings. To estimate annual cost per initial patient when death differs across groups, account for time alive and terminal events. A treatment that extends life may increase accumulated admissions despite a lower recurrent rate while alive. A joint model of recurrent events and death, or a mean cumulative event count accounting for terminal death, can help answer that question.

Joint longitudinal-survival models link an evolving measurement, such as a biomarker, with the event hazard using an estimated latent biomarker trajectory or shared random effects. This differs from a simple time-dependent Cox covariate with measurement error ignored. Joint modelling can be useful when repeated measurement and dropout or death are related; the link and model assumptions matter. [1 pp. 29–30; 16]

Excel: one transparent engine

Create a row for each time point. Cell Formula Meaning

R: reproducible statistical estimation and model inputs

The companion file Hazard_examples.R includes a simulated dataset, KM, NA, Cox, baseline predictions, diagnostics, parametric curves, changing HRs, AH and a matrix example. It also includes a simulation of a non-PH trial to demonstrate dependence on follow-up. The following core excerpt can run with base R:

apply_hr <- function(t, s, hr) {
  stopifnot(length(t) >= 2, length(t) == length(s), length(hr) == length(t)-1,
            all(is.finite(t)), all(is.finite(s)), all(is.finite(hr)),
            t[1] == 0, abs(s[1]-1) < 1e-10, all(diff(t) > 0),
            all(s >= 0 & s <= 1), all(diff(s) <= 0), all(hr > 0))
  out <- numeric(length(s)); out[1] <- s[1]
  for (i in 2:length(s)) {
    if (s[i-1] == 0 || s[i] == 0) out[i] <- 0
    else out[i] <- out[i-1] * (s[i]/s[i-1])^hr[i-1]
  }
  out
}
apply_hr(0:3, c(1,.8,.6,.5), c(.7,1,1))

The function requires a valid time grid and reference curve. stopifnot() stops on invalid inputs rather than silently repairing them. out stores the new survival estimate and starts at one. The loop calculates each interval relative survival, applies that interval’s HR and multiplies by the previous adjusted value. The zero branch avoids undefined ratios and carries forward absorbing zero. hr[i-1] means the HR is supplied per interval, not per time point. The call returns the Chapter 19 example. This function explicitly handles only a positive, piecewise-constant HR on a coherent single-event survival curve.

To fit a Cox regression in R, survival::coxph(Surv(time,status) ~ trt + risk, data=d) creates the outcome from observed times and event indicators; the right side lists predictors. exp(coef(fit)) converts coefficients to HRs. cox.zph(fit) assesses changing coefficients. basehaz(fit, centered=FALSE) returns estimated baseline cumulative hazards (all covariates zero); survfit(fit,newdata=...) predicts survival for chosen profiles. For standardised survival, predict separately for each target profile with both treatment values, then average probabilities. The companion script shows the calculation, rather than treating newdata with mean values as marginalisation.

35. Uncertainty and validation

A point estimate supplies one possible hazard curve. A confidence interval quantifies uncertainty in estimation under the fitted model; an extrapolation scenario describes uncertainty about assumptions not learned fully from the trial. These are different sources of uncertainty.

Suppose HR=0.70 with reported 95% CI 0.50–0.98. Approximate the log standard error from the bounds. Sample log HR from a normal distribution with mean log(0.70) and that standard error, then exponentiate. This gives positive HRs and a distribution consistent with an approximately normal log estimator; it does not imply a normal HR. If the reference survival and HR were estimated jointly, sample their coefficients jointly using the estimated covariance or posterior draws, or bootstrap the relevant analysis. Sampling separate unrelated normal parameters can break necessary correlations or create implausible curves.

For each probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) draw, rebuild survival from the sampled hazards, integrate life years, multiply state time by utilities and costs, and calculate incremental net monetary benefit (NMB = willingness-to-pay × incremental QALYs − incremental costs). NMB avoids instability of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) when incremental QALYs are near zero. The proportion of draws with positive incremental NMB estimates the probability of cost-effectiveness under the model and its specified parameter distributions. Structural scenarios such as PH versus non-PH and alternative waning should be separately described, not buried in a confidence interval for one HR.

Worked audit example

A model has S(reference, year 1)=0.8 and S(reference, year 2)=0.6. The HR changes from 0.7 to 1 at year 1. Incorrectly using direct pointwise powering gives treated year-2 survival 0.6. Correct interval chaining gives about 0.642. For 1,000 starting patients, the erroneous spreadsheet removes about 42 expected survivors at the year-2 time point. It does not follow that the model lost 42 life years: life years are an area under the survival curve. Integrate the entire correct and incorrect curves to quantify that effect, then reflect state time and costs. Chapter 28 gives a complete economic calculation.

Validation checks

  • Check the HR refers to the correct numerator, denominator, endpoint, time origin, population and adjustment scale.
  • Check hazards are nonnegative, cumulative hazards non-decreasing and survival non-increasing within [0,1]. Confirm zero handling and time-unit conversions.
  • Check that competing-exit probabilities and end-of-cycle transition probabilities have the correct interpretation and sum to one as appropriate.
  • Check OS≥PFS and, where TOT means alive and receiving treatment, OS≥TOT. Inspect coherence rather than automatically clipping.
  • Verify life table adjustments do not double count death and cure model adjustments do not inadvertently change the cure fraction.
  • Recalculate survival both via cumulative hazard and interval chaining. Under constant HR, the direct-power rule must agree with chaining.
  • When HR becomes 1, conditional survival must match reference and the survival ratio must stop changing. Previously accumulated survival benefit must not be erased.
  • Review hazard plots, external risk predictions, clinically plausible tail behaviour and results under grid refinement.
  • Inspect all PSA draws for validity and preserve correlated uncertainty. A successful numerical test does not validate the clinical assumption.

I. Decision path

  1. Define the question: event rate, cumulative risk, mean time, expected count, or lifetime economic outputs.
  2. Determine whether the outcome is a single first event, a competing first event, a recurrent event or a transition between states.
  3. Establish time zero, time unit, risk set and treatment strategy.
  4. Identify the estimand and whether the effect is conditional or marginal; address measured confounding and relevant censoring assumptions.
  5. Evaluate hazard shape, PH and modelling alternatives. Choose a suitable estimand and supported time horizon.
  6. In HEOR, check that the reference risk and relative effect refer to the same estimand before combining them. If they do not, re-estimate or model the compatible quantities rather than force the transformation.
  7. Validate the resulting occupancy, survival and cost predictions. Report the sensitivity to assumptions.

L. Exercises with worked solutions

1. A hazard of 0.5 per year is constant. What is the six-month probability of death? Why is it not 25%? Solution: 1−e−0.25=22.12%1-e^{-0.25}=22.12\%. Death changes the risk set through the interval; 0.25 is cumulative hazard, not probability.

2. For the ten-patient dataset, what is KM survival at month 4? Solution: use the product of the factors 9/10, 8/9, 6/7 and 5/6. The censor at month 2.5 changes the denominator for month 3. The result is 0.57143. This differs from the raw fraction still observed alive.

3. Calculate the AH at six months from the same KM curve. Solution: KM survival is 0.428571 at month 6. The step-function area is 1 × 1.000000 + 1 × 0.900000 + 1 × 0.800000 + 1 × 0.685714 + 1 × 0.571429 + 1 × 0.428571 = 4.385714 months. AH = (1−0.428571)/4.385714 = 0.130293 deaths/person-month.

4. Reference survival is 0.4 at five years. What is treated survival under a constant HR of 0.5? Solution: 0.4=0.63246\sqrt{0.4}=0.63246. Reference death risk is 0.6; treatment risk is 0.36754; mortality risk reduction is 38.74%, not 50%.

5. Survival at years 1 and 2 is 0.8 and 0.6, HR=0.5 in year 1 and 1 in year 2. Solution: treated year-1 survival is 0.89443; year-2 survival is 0.89443(0.6/0.8)=0.670820.89443(0.6/0.8)=0.67082. Direct powering with HR=1 would incorrectly give 0.6.

6. If two subgroups share HR=0.5, why can the overall HR differ after follow-up? Solution: treatment alters subgroup survivor weights, so each marginal hazard is a different weighted average of subgroup rates. No baseline confounding is required.

7. A progression hazard is unchanged but a competing death hazard falls. What happens to first progression probability? Solution: it may increase because more patients remain at risk; with constant rates and positive progression rate this follows from the competing-risk formula. It does not establish that progression biology worsened.

8. A published RAH is 0.75 at 12 months. Can it generate a comparator curve? Solution: not without extra assumptions or the underlying hazards/curves. It is a ratio of summary incidence rates over the stated horizon. Under exponential arm distributions it equals their constant HR; that is a special case.

9. A treatment’s OS HR is 0.8. Should we multiply background mortality by 0.8? Solution: only if the modelling assumption correctly represents that effect on total mortality. If the treatment acts solely on excess mortality, use separate components; the observed all-cause HR cannot be reinterpreted as an excess HR without supporting information.

10. A model has correct observed five-year calibration but an implausible hazard at year 15. Should it be accepted? Solution: no automatic acceptance. Calibration within observed follow-up does not verify extrapolation. Examine hazard plausibility, external evidence and sensitivity to alternative tails.
