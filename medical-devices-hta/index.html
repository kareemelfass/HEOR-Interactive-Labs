01What is a medical device? (WHO definition)
Key idea: a medical device works mainly by physical or mechanical means, not by drug, immune or metabolic action.

The WHO definition (WHO, Health technology assessment of medical devices, second edition, 2025, Glossary, pages ix to x) [1]:

Medical device: Any instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, appliance, implant, reagent for in vitro use, software, material or other similar or related article intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for human beings for one or more of the specific medical purpose(s) of:

  • diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of disease;
  • diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury;
  • investigation, replacement, modification or support of the anatomy or physiological process;
  • supporting or sustaining life;
  • control of conception;
  • cleaning, disinfection or sterilization of medical devices;
  • providing information by means of in vitro examination of specimens derived from the human body;
  • and does not achieve its primary intended action by pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic means, in or on the human body, but which may be assisted in its intended function by such means.

The WHO note on borderline products [1]

Products which may be considered to be medical devices in some jurisdictions but not in others include: disinfection substances, aids for persons with disabilities, devices incorporating animal and/or human tissues, devices for in-vitro fertilization or assisted reproduction technologies.

In vitro diagnostic (IVD) device (WHO Glossary) [1]

'In Vitro Diagnostic (IVD) medical device' means a medical device, whether used alone or in combination, intended by the manufacturer for the in-vitro examination of specimens derived from the human body solely or principally to provide information for diagnostic, monitoring or compatibility purposes.

“In vitro” means outside the body, for example in a laboratory.

How to read the definition: three tests

  1. Is it intended by the manufacturer for a medical purpose? The intended use decides, not the object itself.
  2. Does it fit one of the listed purposes? Diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, support of anatomy, life support, contraception, cleaning medical devices, or lab testing of human specimens.
  3. Is its main action NOT pharmacological, immunological or metabolic? If the main action is chemical in the body, it is a medicine, even if a device delivers it.

Combination products (WHO Glossary) [1]: a product that combines two or more types of medical products (for example a medicine and a device) into one product. HTA of these needs to consider both parts.

02Discounting and high up-front costs
Key idea: discounting never shrinks a cost paid today, but it does shrink health benefits that come later. So the higher the discount rate, the worse a technology with a large up-front cost looks.

Terms

  • Discounting: converting future costs and benefits into today's value, because a benefit or payment now is valued more than the same one later.
  • Discount rate (r): the yearly rate used for this conversion. A review of European guidelines recommends country-specific rates; without local guidance, EUnetHTA recommends 3% to 5%, with a wider range of 0% to 10% explored in sensitivity analysis [2].
  • ICER (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio): extra cost divided by extra health gain, here cost per QALY.
  • QALY (quality-adjusted life year): one year of life in full health; a year in poorer health counts as less than one.

The mechanism

  • A device is often paid for at the start (year 0). Year 0 is not discounted, so its cost stays the same whatever the rate.
  • Its health benefits come later (years 1 to 5), so they are discounted.
  • A drug taken every year has costs and benefits spread over time, so both shrink together.
  • Result: the higher the rate, the more the device's ICER rises compared with the drug's.

Worked example

Both options cost US$10,000 in total (undiscounted) and give 0.2 QALYs a year for 5 years (1.0 QALY undiscounted). Device: US$10,000 paid at year 0. Drug: US$2,000 paid at the end of each year, years 1 to 5. QALYs are counted at the end of each year, years 1 to 5.

How the 5% row is calculated

  • Annuity factor = (1 - 1.05-5) / 0.05 = 4.3295
  • QALYs today = 0.2 x 4.3295 = 0.8659
  • Device ICER = 10,000 / 0.8659 = US$11,549
  • Drug cost today = 2,000 x 4.3295 = 8,659, so drug ICER = 8,659 / 0.8659 = US$10,000

What it shows

  • At 0%, both are identical.
  • At 3%, 5% and 8%, the device ICER is about 9%, 15% and 25% higher, with the same total spending and the same health gain.
  • Why this matters for devices: implants, capital equipment and one-off procedures carry most of their cost at the start. The choice of discount rate can move a device across a cost-effectiveness threshold.
  • Practical point: always report results at several discount rates for up-front technologies.

03Classes of medical devices
Key idea: regulators group devices by risk. Higher risk means stricter evidence rules before the device can be sold. Classification is a regulatory step, not an HTA step, but it shapes how much evidence an HTA will find.

What drives the risk class (WHO) [1]

Regulators classify medical devices according to factors such as the potential level of hazard and risk to patients, with risk being defined in relation to the duration of use, degree of invasiveness, whether the device is implantable or non-implantable, or whether it is active or contains an active substance.

General examples (WHO) [1]

Different countries have different risk classification systems, but in general, simple devices such as thermometers are categorized as low risk, while devices such as hearing aids and infusion pumps for intravenous medications are classified as medium risk, and devices such as implanted stimulators as high risk.

Why the class matters for HTA

  • Evidence depth: in the EU, clinical investigations (studies in people) are required for implantable and class III devices, with listed exceptions. The regulation does not require these studies to be randomised [7].
  • Which devices get an HTA: WHO notes that, unlike most medicines, low-risk devices are not required to undergo HTA [1].
  • Regulatory data is not enough for HTA: WHO states that clinical data from the regulatory process will not be sufficient for HTA [1], because HTA asks about effectiveness against current care and value for money.

04Matching, propensity scores and counterfactual effects
Key idea: when there is no randomised trial, we compare treated patients with similar untreated patients, to estimate what would have happened to the treated patients without the device.

Why this matters for devices

  • Device trials are often small, short or not randomised, so HTA often relies on real-world evidence (RWE): evidence from routine care data such as registries and claims.
  • Daubner-Bendes et al. recommend relying on RWE when trial evidence is limited, and add [8]:
However, appropriate methods of bias-adjustment (e.g. counterfactual effect estimation) need to be applied.

Terms, in plain words

  • Counterfactual: the outcome the same patient would have had under the other option. We never see it directly, so we estimate it.
  • Confounding: a factor (for example disease severity) that affects both who gets the device and the outcome. It makes a raw comparison misleading.
  • Propensity score (PS): each patient's probability of getting the device, given their characteristics (age, risk, and so on). It squeezes many characteristics into one number [9].
  • Matching: pairing each device patient with an untreated patient who has a similar propensity score.
  • Balance check: after matching, the groups should look alike. A common rule is a standardised mean difference (the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation) below 0.1 for each characteristic [10].
  • ATT (average treatment effect on the treated): the effect of the device among the patients who actually received it.

Worked example: one confounder, checked by hand

Device patients tend to be low risk. Outcome: complication rate.

Risk groupDevice patientsDevice complication rateStandard care patientsStandard care complication rate
High risk2030%8035%
Low risk8010%2012%

Step 1. Naive comparison (misleading)

  • Device: (20 x 30% + 80 x 10%) / 100 = (6 + 8) / 100 = 14.0%
  • Standard care: (80 x 35% + 20 x 12%) / 100 = (28 + 2.4) / 100 = 30.4%
  • Naive difference: 14.0% - 30.4% = -16.4 percentage points. The device looks very good.

Step 2. Why that is wrong

Most device patients are low risk, and most standard care patients are high risk. The comparison mixes the device effect with the risk difference (confounding).

Step 3. Build the counterfactual for device patients

  • Ask: what complication rate would the same 100 device patients have had with standard care?
  • Use the standard care rates within each risk group, weighted by the device patients' mix (20 high, 80 low).
  • Counterfactual = (20 x 35% + 80 x 12%) / 100 = (7 + 9.6) / 100 = 16.6%

Step 4. The adjusted effect (ATT)

  • ATT = 14.0% - 16.6% = -2.6 percentage points
  • The device still helps, but about 6 times less than the naive comparison suggested.

Step 5. Where the propensity score fits

Here the propensity score is simply the share of each risk group that got the device: 0.20 in high risk (20 of 100) and 0.80 in low risk (80 of 100). Matching on the propensity score is the same as comparing within risk groups. With many characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities, centre), the propensity score does this in one step [9].

Example R code (for analysts)

library(MatchIt)                                    # 1
m <- matchit(device ~ age + sex + risk_score + centre_volume,
             data = df,
             method = "nearest",                    # 2
             distance = "glm", link = "linear.logit",  # 3
             caliper = 0.2,                         # 4
             estimand = "ATT")                      # 5
summary(m)                                          # 6
md <- match.data(m)                                 # 7
fit <- lm(complication ~ device, data = md, weights = weights)  # 8
lmtest::coeftest(fit, vcov. = sandwich::vcovCL, cluster = ~subclass)  # 9

What the code does (high level): it estimates each patient's propensity score, pairs each device patient with the closest untreated patient, checks that the pairs are similar, and then compares complication rates within the matched sample.

Line by line

  1. Loads the MatchIt package, which does propensity score matching in R.
  2. matchit(...) builds the matched sample. The formula says: model the chance of device (1 = got the device, 0 = not) from age, sex, risk score and centre volume. method = "nearest" pairs each device patient with the nearest control.
  3. distance = "glm" estimates the propensity score with a logistic regression. link = "linear.logit" measures closeness on the logit scale (the log-odds), which is the scale the caliper rule refers to.
  4. caliper = 0.2 refuses pairs that are further apart than 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score, a common rule of thumb [10].
  5. estimand = "ATT" asks for the effect in patients who received the device.
  6. summary(m) prints balance statistics, including standardised mean differences, before and after matching.
  7. match.data(m) returns the matched patients only, with a weights column and a subclass column (the pair ID).
  8. lm(...) regresses the 0/1 complication outcome on device use in the matched data. The device coefficient is the risk difference (ATT).
  9. Computes standard errors that allow for the pairing (clustered by subclass), which gives confidence intervals accounting for matched pairs.

Device-specific cautions

  • Operator and centre effects: outcomes depend on who uses the device and where. Include centre or operator volume, or cluster by centre.
  • Learning curve and calendar time: early cases may do worse. Include year of procedure or operator experience.
  • Device version: check that all patients received the same (or equivalent) version.
  • Unmeasured confounding: matching only balances what is measured. Report a sensitivity analysis such as the E-value (how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away the effect) [11].

05What national guidelines say about medical devices
Key idea: most economic evaluation guidelines were written for medicines. A 2025 systematic review searched for device-specific advice and found it in only a few national guidelines, covering only some device features [12].

What the review found (Akpinar et al., 2025) [12]

  • The authors reviewed 77 grey literature sources and economic evaluation guidelines.
  • Relevant information for only five medical device-specific features was found in six guidelines.
  • None of the guidelines addressed the domains of organizational impact or transferability.

06Economies of scale: cost per case and volume
Key idea: a device with a large fixed cost becomes cheaper per patient the more patients it treats, until it hits capacity and a second machine is needed.

Terms

  • Fixed cost: cost that does not change with the number of patients in a year (the machine, its maintenance contract).
  • Variable cost: cost that rises with each patient (single-use consumables).
  • Average cost per case: total yearly cost divided by number of cases.
  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as volume rises, because the fixed cost is shared by more patients.
  • Utilisation: how much of the machine's capacity is actually used.

What the sources say

  • Daubner-Bendes et al. [8]: at hospitals, equipment is a fixed (capital) cost, while consumables are variable costs, and As the average cost is dependent on the economies of scale, it is not easy to calculate the actual cost per intervention of complex MDs with high upfront costs and unpredictable maintenance costs.
  • Daubner-Bendes et al. [8]: economies of scale in large specialised centres accelerates the adoption of MDs and allows institutes to employ more specialised operators and ancillary staff, which ultimately improves outcomes and reduces the average cost per case.
  • Dutch guideline, quoted in Akpinar et al. [12]: depreciation charges per procedure are influenced by the utilization degree, or in other terms, the non-optimal use of the device.
  • WHO [1]: some countries are moving towards centralized procurement to take advantage of economies of scale. The latter may not; however, be suitable for countries with diverse health needs.
  • Policy implication (Daubner-Bendes Recommendation 2.1 [8]): national HTA should use average expected payments, rather than actual costs, because actual costs depend on each hospital's volume and economies of scale.

Worked example

  • Capital: US$550,000 (machine 500,000 + installation and training 50,000), life 5 years, discount rate 8%.
  • Annual capital cost (annuity): 550,000 / 3.99271 = US$137,751.
  • Maintenance: US$25,000 a year per machine.
  • Fixed cost per machine per year: 137,751 + 25,000 = US$162,751.
  • Consumables: US$300 per case.
  • Capacity: 1,000 cases a year per machine.

Formula: machines needed = volume / 1,000, rounded up. Cost per case = (machines x 162,751) / volume + 300.

  • Going from 100 to 1,000 cases cuts the cost per case from US$1,927.51 to US$462.75 (a 76% drop).

07NPV and IRR: the buyer's view
Key idea: a hospital buying a device asks a money question: “Will what we are paid per case cover the purchase, the running costs and the return we need?” NPV and IRR answer it. They measure money, not health, so they sit beside the ICER and never replace it.

Terms

  • Cash flow: money in minus money out in a year.
  • Discounting: converting future money into today's money (see Section 2).
  • NPV (net present value): the sum of all cash flows, each converted to today's value. Above zero means the project earns more than the discount rate.
  • IRR (internal rate of return): the discount rate at which NPV is exactly zero. It is the project's own yearly return. Compare it with the return you require (the "hurdle rate").
  • Payback period: years until the cash received equals the cash paid (not discounted).

Why it matters for devices: Daubner-Bendes et al. recommend that national HTA use average expected payments, rather than actual costs [8]. The payment level per case is exactly what decides whether a hospital's investment pays back.

What the example shows

  • Payment per case decides adoption. A cut of US$1,000 per case turns a good investment into a loss.
  • A payer can ask: “What payment makes NPV zero?” Here, about US$5,216.
  • Volume matters too (see Section 6): below about 69% of planned cases, the investment does not pay back.

When to trust NPV or IRR: decision steps

  1. One project, yes or no? Both give the same answer for conventional cash flows.
  2. Choosing between projects, or only one can be funded? Use NPV. IRR ignores size:
    • Project A: invest US$100,000, receive US$60,000 a year for 3 years → IRR 36.3%, NPV at 8% US$54,626.
    • Project B: invest US$1,000,000, receive US$450,000 a year for 3 years → IRR 16.6%, NPV at 8% US$159,694.
    • IRR picks A; NPV picks B, which creates more value.
  3. Cash flows change sign more than once (for example a big mid-life upgrade)? Use NPV: there can be more than one IRR.
  4. Talking to non-finance people? Use IRR as the headline, with NPV beside it.

Cautions

  • Money, not health: a device can pass the NPV test and fail the ICER test, or the reverse.
  • Simple model: tax, inflation and resale value are left out here.
  • Spreadsheet tip: Excel's NPV function treats the first value as year 1, so add the year 0 purchase separately [13].

08Depreciation, annuity and the double-counting trap
Key idea: a device is paid for once. You can show that cost at year 0 or as equal yearly annuity payments. Both give the same answer. Adding depreciation on top of either one counts the device twice.

Terms

  • Capital cost: the one-off cost of buying and installing the device.
  • Depreciation: an accounting way to spread a cost already paid over the years of use. Straight-line depreciation uses equal slices with no interest.
  • Annuity (equivalent annual cost): equal yearly payments whose value today equals the price. They include the cost of having money tied up (interest).
  • Book value: the cost minus the depreciation so far.
  • Capital charge: interest on the book value.

Step 0: the same device and the same rate

  • Capital: US$550,000 paid at year 0 (price 500,000 + installation and training 50,000).
  • Life: 5 years. Discount rate: 8%.
  • Running costs: US$325,000 a year (maintenance 25,000 + consumables 300 x 1,000 cases).
  • Volume: 1,000 cases a year.

Step 1: the building block, the discount factor

  • Discount factor = 1 / (1 + r)t. It converts a future US$1 into today's money.
  • Why it works: US$1 today grows to US$1.08 in one year. So US$1.08 next year is worth US$1 today.

  • The annuity factor is just the sum of the discount factors for years 1 to 5.
  • 0.92593 + 0.85734 + 0.79383 + 0.73503 + 0.68058 = 3.99271
  • Meaning: US$1 paid every year for 5 years is worth US$3.99271 today, not US$5.
  • The annuity payment is the yearly amount whose value today equals the price: payment x 3.99271 = 550,000; payment = 550,000 / 3.99271 = US$137,751.

Step 6: the double-counting mistake (Method D)

  • What the analyst did: put US$550,000 in year 0, and added straight-line depreciation of US$110,000 a year, discounted.
  • Capital cost in the model: 550,000 + 439,198 = US$989,198.
  • Why this is wrong: the US$550,000 at year 0 is the whole purchase. Depreciation is only that same purchase described year by year. You paid for the device once, yet the model charges for it 1.8 times.

Step 8: the one idea that ties it together

  • Principal part of each annuity payment (from the loan table) is the true economic depreciation. It is smaller in early years and larger in later years: 93,751, 101,251, 109,351, 118,099, 127,547.
  • Interest part is the capital charge.
  • Annuity = depreciation + capital charge, in a version that already includes the time value of money.
  • Straight-line depreciation is a rougher, interest-free version of that, which is why it needs a capital charge to be correct.

Which method to use: decision steps

  1. Need a cost per case or per year? Use the annuity (B), or depreciation plus capital charge (E). No year-0 line.
  2. Building a model over several years? Put the purchase at year 0 (A). No depreciation.
  3. Doing a budget impact analysis? Record the cash in the year it is paid, not discounted, and no depreciation.
  4. Never combine A with depreciation. That gives D.

What the sources say: the Adams et al. checklist, quoted in Akpinar et al., asks Was depreciation considered appropriately and measured correctly? [12]. The sources do not give a formula; the methods above are standard financial maths (own synthesis).

09Transferring real-world evidence to late-adopter countries
Key idea: countries that adopt devices late (for example in Central and Eastern Europe, and many middle-income countries) often have no local data yet. Real-world evidence from early-adopter countries can help, but only after checking that the device is the same, the study is good enough, and the setting is similar.

The recommendation (Daubner-Bendes et al., Recommendation #1.2 [8]), verbatim

Explore the feasibility of transferring RWE to late technology adopter countries in a stepwise approach. In order to overcome the uncertain quality and local relevance of RWD, a stepwise approach is recommended to explore the feasibility of transferring international RWE to CEE countries. The first step should be the systematic search and collection of potentially relevant RWEs, followed by an evaluation of the equivalence of technologies presented in the study of origin and local context. As a result, studies in which the technologies have no major differences should be channelled into further assessment. In the next step, the quality of evidence should be evaluated by using quality assessment tools, such as GRADE, ROBINS-I or the ISPOR initiative on RWE transparency and include only studies with appropriate quality for quantitative synthesis (e.g., meta-analysis). As a last step to guarantee applicability of evidence in local jurisdiction, use of a transferability checklist [e.g., EUnetHTA Adaptation Toolkit] should be considered to evaluate the variation in patient population, medical practise and health systems. In CEE countries with capacity constraints in the follow-up care and heterogeneous patient pathways, highly conservative effectiveness estimates should be applied. On the other hand, limited variability in the real-world outcomes across centres in the country of origin indicates less dependence on local factors, and consequently increased transferability of high-quality RWE with appropriate comparator can be assumed to late technology adopter countries.

Reference numbers inside the original quotation were removed for readability.

Terms

  • RWD (real-world data): data from routine care, such as registries, claims and electronic health records.
  • RWE (real-world evidence): evidence produced by analysing RWD.
  • CEE: Central and Eastern Europe.
  • Transferability: whether results from one country apply in another.

The four steps, in plain words

  1. Search and collect. Systematically find all potentially relevant RWE studies.
  2. Check the technology is equivalent. Is the device (and version) in the study the same as the one used locally? Keep only studies with no major differences.
  3. Check the quality. Rate each study with a quality tool and keep only those good enough to pool (for example in a meta-analysis):
    • GRADE: rates the certainty of a body of evidence [14].
    • ROBINS-I: rates risk of bias in a non-randomised study [15].
    • ISPOR RWE transparency initiative: good practice for transparent real-world studies [16].
  4. Check applicability to the local setting. Use a transferability checklist, such as the EUnetHTA HTA Adaptation Toolkit [17], to compare patients, medical practice and health system.

Two decision cues from the recommendation

  • Be conservative when the local system has limited follow-up capacity or very different patient pathways: use highly conservative effectiveness estimates.
  • Be more confident when outcomes in the country of origin vary little between centres: results depend less on local factors, so high-quality RWE with an appropriate comparator transfers better.

Link to Section 4: step 3 is where bias adjustment (matching, propensity scores, counterfactual effect estimation) is judged. A study that did not adjust for confounding should not pass the quality step.
