01 · Model structure

State occupancy versus state transitions

A partitioned survival model (PSM) computes how many patients occupy PF, PD and death from marginal PFS and OS curves. A state-transition model (STM) specifies how patients move between those states.

PSM directly from PFS and OS

\[PF(t)=S_{PFS}(t),\quad PD(t)=S_{OS}(t)-S_{PFS}(t),\quad Death(t)=1-S_{OS}(t)\]

For example, if month-12 PFS is 50% and OS is 75%, occupancy is 50% PF, 25% PD and 25% dead. Nothing here identifies the pathway followed by the patients who died.

STM requires three transition processes

\[PF\xrightarrow{\lambda_P(t)}PD,\qquad PF\xrightarrow{\lambda_D(t)}Death,\qquad PD\xrightarrow{\mu(u,t_P)}Death\]

\(t\) is time since model entry, \(u\) is time since progression and \(t_P\) is the time progression occurred. The model may or may not need to retain both clocks.

02 · Time-to-event fundamentals

Why TTP is a survival curve but is not PF occupancy

A survival curve reports the estimated probability of not yet experiencing its defined event. “Survival” does not always mean “still alive”.

The TTP Kaplan–Meier (KM) calculation excludes pre-progression death as an event and removes the patient from the progression risk set. It supplies information on the cause-specific progression hazard, not the observed cumulative progression proportion in a population also exposed to death.

Always verify the actual study protocol and endpoint rules. TTP death censoring, scan schedules, post-progression treatment and time origins must be compatible with your model.
03 · Core derivation

Recover PF → death using PFS and TTP

While PF, a patient can progress or die before progression. These are competing first events. The total instantaneous exit rate is the sum of their cause-specific hazards.

\[h_{PFS}(t)=\lambda_P(t)+\lambda_D(t),\qquad h_{TTP}(t)=\lambda_P(t)\]
\[\boxed{\lambda_D(t)=h_{PFS}(t)-h_{TTP}(t)}\]

For fitted survival functions, the cumulative hazard is \(H(t)=-\ln S(t)\). Differencing compatible cumulative hazards yields the accumulated pre-progression death hazard.

\[H_D(t)=H_{PFS}(t)-H_{TTP}(t)=-\ln S_{PFS}(t)+\ln S_{TTP}(t)\]

Six-month values worked by hand

Let \(S_{PFS}(6)=0.50\), \(S_{TTP}(6)=0.70\).

\[H_{PFS}=-\ln(0.50)=0.693147;\qquad H_{TTP}=-\ln(0.70)=0.356675\]
\[H_D=0.693147-0.356675=0.336472\]

If and only if hazards are assumed constant over those six months:

\[\lambda_P=0.356675/6=0.059446;\qquad\lambda_D=0.336472/6=0.056079\]

With complete curves, derive hazard increments separately for each cycle. A single six-month point cannot establish what happened within those six months.

04 · Avoid the wrong subtraction

Why TTP minus PFS does not equal deaths before progression

Cause-specific hazards add. Their corresponding survival percentages do not. The probability of pre-progression death must also account for patients who progress before they can experience that competing first event.

The cumulative incidence function (CIF) measures the actual probability of experiencing a specified first event before competing first events, accounting for the probability of still being PF at every instant.

\[CIF_D(t)=\int_0^t S_{PFS}(u)\lambda_D(u)\,du;\qquad CIF_P(t)=\int_0^t S_{PFS}(u)\lambda_P(u)\,du\]
\[S_{PFS}(t)+CIF_D(t)+CIF_P(t)=1\]

For constant hazards and \(a=\lambda_P+\lambda_D\):

\[CIF_D(t)=\frac{\lambda_D}{a}(1-e^{-at});\quad CIF_P(t)=\frac{\lambda_P}{a}(1-e^{-at})\]

Our example at month 12

\[S_{TTP}(12)=e^{-0.08(12)}=0.3829;\quad S_{PFS}(12)=e^{-0.12(12)}=0.2369\]
\[\underbrace{0.3829-0.2369}_{\text{incorrect death probability}}=0.1460;\quad \underbrace{\frac{0.04}{0.12}(1-e^{-1.44})}_{\text{correct pre-PD death CIF}}=0.2544\]
Three distinct quantities: \(1-S_{TTP}\) is net progression failure, \(CIF_P\) is the observed chance of progression as the first exit from PF, and \(PD(t)\) is actual progressed-and-alive occupancy after PD mortality. They are not interchangeable.
05 · Implement PF transitions

Convert competing hazards into monthly probabilities

Rates are not probabilities. The competing-hazards conversion integrates the two potential exits over the full cycle while respecting their common risk set.

Let \(\Delta\) be cycle length and \(a=\lambda_P+\lambda_D\). For constant hazards within a cycle:

\[\begin{aligned}p_{PF,PF}&=e^{-a\Delta}\\p^{first-exit}_{PF,PD}&=\frac{\lambda_P}{a}(1-e^{-a\Delta})\\p^{first-exit}_{PF,Death}&=\frac{\lambda_D}{a}(1-e^{-a\Delta})\end{aligned}\]

For \(\lambda_P=0.08,\lambda_D=0.04,\Delta=1\):

\[p_{PF,PF}=0.886920;\quad p_{PF,PD}=0.075386;\quad p_{PF,Death}=0.037693\]

The values sum to 1 apart from rounding. When total hazard equals zero, remaining PF has probability 1 and both exits zero.

Contrast with a discrete net-TTP allocation

Another approach assigns the TTP net event decrement directly to progression, then assigns the residual PFS decrement to death:

\[p^{net}_{PF,PD}=1-e^{-\lambda_P\Delta};\quad p^{net}_{PF,Death}=e^{-\lambda_P\Delta}-e^{-a\Delta}\]

In the illustrative one-month case this gives 0.076884 progression and 0.036196 death. Both rows sum to 1, but they differ. The net-TTP approach is a discrete allocation rule, not the exact competing-hazards decomposition; investigate its assumptions and shorten cycles in sensitivity analysis.

Advanced: same-cycle PF → PD → death

The formula above allocates the first exit from PF, then the live simulator places new progressors in PD1 at cycle end. It therefore omits PD death immediately following within-cycle progression. With constant within-cycle PD death hazard \(\mu\), the exact probability of being alive in PD after a cycle, starting in PF, is:

\[P(PD_\Delta)=\lambda_P\frac{e^{-\mu\Delta}-e^{-a\Delta}}{a-\mu},\quad a\ne \mu\]

If \(a=\mu\), the limit is \(\lambda_P\Delta e^{-a\Delta}\). The remaining probability after PF and PD occupancy is death, which includes either route. With time-dependent PPS or history effects, more general numerical integration may be needed.

06 · Identification

PSM describes occupancy, but it does not uniquely identify the progression and death histories generating that occupancy.

07 · Post-progression survival

Does mortality depend on duration, history or neither?

Separate time since progression, the elapsed time occupying PD, from time to progression, the time originally spent PF. They may independently predict PPS.

Post-progression hazardStructureReason
Constant for all: \(\mu\)One PD stateNo duration or history memory needed
Varies only with duration: \(\mu(u)\)PD tunnelsDistinguishes month 1 from month 8 after progression
Varies only with progression timing: \(\mu(t_P)\)History groupsEarly and late groups retain different constant hazards
Varies with both: \(\mu(u,t_P)\)History-specific tunnelsTracks both relevant clocks

Compute conditional mortality from PPS

\[q_k=1-\frac{S_{PPS}(k\Delta)}{S_{PPS}((k-1)\Delta)}\]

The conditional denominator is essential. A decrease from 90% PPS to 72% PPS is not a 18% monthly death probability: it is \(1-0.72/0.90=20\%\).

One aggregate PPS curve can describe a changing hazard without identifying whether that change arises from duration, prognosis, progression timing or selection. Do not infer conditional subgroup curves from the aggregate curve alone.
08 · Semi-Markov mechanics

How patients flow through duration-specific tunnels

A tunnel represents a duration interval, not a distinct clinical disease state. New progressors enter PD1 at the end of their progression cycle. In subsequent cycles, survivors move into later tunnels.

\[Deaths_{k,c}=N_{k,c}q_k;\qquad N_{k+1,c+1}=N_{k,c}(1-q_k)\]
PD1First full PD month\(q_1=0.10\)
PD2Second full PD month\(q_2=0.20\)
PD3+Later PD months\(q_3=0.10\)

For 10 new progressors: 1 dies during month one and 9 advance to PD2; 1.8 of those die in month two and 7.2 advance to PD3+. At the same calendar month, other progressors may still occupy PD1, receiving a different hazard.

09 · Both clocks together

If PPS depends on when progression occurred, add history groups

Patients may have identical duration since progression but different mortality because one progressed early and the other late. Simple PD tunnels cannot distinguish them.

Illustrative split: early progression at or before month 6 versus late progression after month 6. These cut-offs and probabilities are hypothetical, not recommended treatment-specific parameters.

PD intervalEarly mortalityLate mortality
First month30%10%
Second month20%5%
Third month onward15%5%
\[q_{g,k}=1-\frac{S_g(k\Delta)}{S_g((k-1)\Delta)}\]

If 20 patients enter early PD1, 6 die and 14 enter early PD2. Next month 2.8 die and 11.2 progress through their early-group tunnels. If 10 enter late PD1, only 1 dies initially; 9 advance and 0.45 die in the second month.

New early progressors always enter the first early tunnel, even if other early progressors are already in their second or third tunnel. Similarly, late progressors enter late PD1 at the end of their progression cycle.

Data requirement: History-specific PPS must be estimated from IPD or suitably detailed conditional evidence. Differences between observed early and late progressors are prognostic associations, not proof that delaying progression causes longer PPS. Treatment can change which patients enter each subgroup.

In a published CheckMate 025 application, Smare and colleagues estimated three conditional PPS curves using progression before 8 weeks, between 8 and 24 weeks, and after 24 weeks. The structure illustrates conditional PPS modelling, not universal thresholds.

11 · Defensible methods

Which structure does the evidence support?

Build the decision around identifiable transitions and supported conditional survival, not around which modelling framework is familiar.

  1. Check endpoint definitions and populations: verify PFS and TTP share progression criteria, entry time and compatible censoring.
  2. Obtain PF mortality: use directly observed transition data or compatible PFS and TTP cause-specific hazards. Never use a difference of raw curves as death incidence.
  3. Inspect PPS: does a constant post-progression hazard adequately represent the conditional survival shape?
  4. Inspect progression history: conditional PPS may differ between early and late progressors. This requires conditional evidence and careful interpretation of selection.
  5. Handle short-cycle limitations: consider within-cycle progression and death, cycle length and tail extrapolation.
  6. Validate outputs: benchmark generated PFS and OS against observed curves, longer follow-up and plausible external evidence.

12 · Consolidation

Exercises, glossary and methodological references

Test the calculations, review the definitions and use the primary sources when adapting the examples to a real submission.

Exercise 1. PFS hazard = 0.12/month and TTP cause-specific hazard = 0.08/month. Calculate PF → death hazard.
Worked solution
\[\lambda_D=h_{PFS}-h_{TTP}=0.12-0.08=0.04\]
Exercise 2. Using 0.08 and 0.04 competing hazards and a one-month cycle, calculate PF → death first-exit probability.
Worked solution
\[p_D=(0.04/0.12)(1-e^{-0.12})=0.037693\]
Exercise 3. PPS is 90% after one month and 72% after two months. Calculate mortality conditional on surviving the first PD month.
Worked solution
\[q_2=1-0.72/0.90=0.20\]
Exercise 4. Among 100 patients, PFS=50% and OS=75% at month 12. How many patients are alive in PD?
Worked solution
\[PD=100(0.75-0.50)=25\]
