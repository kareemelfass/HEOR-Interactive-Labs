1. Why QALYs?

Treatments help patients live longer, live better, or both. Life years capture only the first benefit. Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) capture both.

Two patients live for five years. One experiences persistent pain and difficulty walking; the other has well-controlled disease. Their survival is identical, but their health is not. A QALY weights time by the value of health experienced.

\[1\ \text{year in full health}=1\ \text{QALY}\]
Describe healthElicit preferencesEstimate utilityCombine with timeCompare health and costs

The calculation is simple. Selecting the appropriate utility values and durations is the methodological challenge.

2. What is a utility?

Utility is a preference-based value assigned to a health state. It is anchored at full health (1) and death (0).

Health stateExample utility
Full health1.00
Controlled disease0.85
Symptomatic disease0.60
Death0.00
State valued worse than death−0.20

One year at utility 0.60 contributes 0.60 QALYs. Five years at this value are equivalent to three years in full health under the conventional QALY framework. Utility is not a survival probability, percentage of functioning or patient satisfaction score. Negative values reflect preferences for a health state, not the worth of a person. Zero is the death anchor, not a missing-data code. The lower limit depends on the instrument and valuation method.

3. Direct utility elicitation

Utility elicitation means obtaining preferences through structured tasks. The methods differ in what respondents are asked to trade or choose.

Time Trade-Off (TTO)

Choose between ten years with chronic pain and a shorter period in full health, followed by death in both cases. Adjust the shorter duration until both options are equally desirable (the point of indifference). If this occurs at six years, the utility is 0.60.

\[10u=6(1),\qquad u=\frac{6}{10}=0.60\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Change the number of full-health years at indifference. The shaded areas are equal.

Conventional TTO values states better than death. Composite TTO uses additional lead time for states valued worse than death. Time preferences and willingness to trade longevity influence responses.

Standard Gamble (SG)

Compare remaining in impaired health with certainty against a hypothetical gamble between full health and immediate death. The duration if alive is held fixed. At indifference, under expected utility theory, the probability of full health is the utility.

\[u=p(1)+(1-p)(0)=p\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Adjust the hypothetical probability of full health at indifference.

SG can reflect attitudes to risk as well as health preferences.

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

Respondents rate health on a scale. A score of 65 on EQ-VAS cannot automatically become utility 0.65: its anchors are worst and best imaginable health, not death and full health.

Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE)

Respondents repeatedly choose between profiles, such as severe pain with good mobility versus mild pain with poor mobility. A statistical model estimates the relative importance of the attributes. Without duration or death, a standard DCE does not directly yield QALY-compatible utilities. Duration-based designs or TTO anchoring provide this link.

TTO trades time; SG trades risk; VAS rates health; DCE compares profiles. Different tasks can yield different values.

4. Measuring health versus valuing health

The patient describes health. A preference-based scoring system supplies the value assigned to that health.

Instead of eliciting preferences at every trial visit, a standardised questionnaire records health-related quality of life (HRQoL). A value set or tariff, estimated from valuation tasks in a sample of respondents, assigns utility to each profile. Selected health profiles are valued directly and a statistical model estimates values for the remaining profiles. The target jurisdiction determines whose preferences are appropriate. [1,2]

Mapping predicts utility from another instrument or clinical outcome using a statistical model. A suitable validated algorithm is needed, and the prediction introduces additional uncertainty.

5. From EQ-5D profile to utility

The five digits of an EQ-5D-5L profile describe severity in five dimensions. They are not numbers to average.

Each dimension has five levels, so there are 3,125 possible profiles (243 for EQ-5D-3L). Profile 31332 describes moderate mobility problems, no self-care problems, moderate usual-activity and pain problems, and slight anxiety/depression.

The revised Egyptian EQ-5D-5L valuation study used composite TTO and selected model 4. Each coefficient gives the decrement relative to level 1. [3, Table 2]

\[u=1-\sum_{d=1}^{5}b_{d,l_d}\]
\[u_{31332}=1-(0.208+0+0.078+0.106+0.054)=0.554\]

The same profile can receive different utilities under different tariffs. The health description is unchanged; the valuation preferences differ. Use the appropriate target-jurisdiction value set and avoid mixing tariffs between arms.

6. Selecting utility evidence

A utility must match the model's population, health state and decision framework, not simply be available in a publication.

  1. Check whether relevant clinical trials collected preference-based HRQoL data. Apply the appropriate scoring approach.
  2. Decide whether treatment-specific or health-state-specific utilities are justified. For repeated observations, consider appropriate regression models accounting for within-patient dependence and covariates.
  3. If trial data are unavailable, assess published utilities for disease severity, population, health-state definitions, measurement timing, instrument, tariff and uncertainty.
  4. Consider validated mapping or targeted vignette valuation when important health states are otherwise not represented.
  5. Determine whether age, comorbidities or adverse events are already captured.

Progression status may not fully explain health quality in oncology. Time to death, treatment exposure and toxicity may be important. Test alternative structures if supported. [1,2]

7. Constant utility and time

QALYs equal the area of a rectangle: its height is utility and its width is time in years.

\[Q=u\,t\]

At utility 0.554, two years produce 1.108 QALYs and six months produce 0.277. Negative utility generates negative QALYs for the relevant period.

8. Changing utility over time

When health changes, calculate QALYs for each period, then add the contributions.

One year at 0.60, two years at 0.80 and two years at 0.40 produce three QALYs across five years.

\[Q=\sum_{i=1}^n u_i\Delta t_i=0.60(1)+0.80(2)+0.40(2)=3.00\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Build a patient journey by changing the three state utilities and durations.

After death, utility is zero. For continuous change, accumulate the signed area under the trajectory.

\[Q=\int_0^T u(t)\,dt\]

9. QALYs from clinical trials

Utility is observed at visits rather than continuously. Linear interpolation assumes a gradual change between adjacent measurements.

For utilities 0.60 at baseline, 0.80 at month six and 0.90 at month twelve, calculate the area under the curve using the trapezoidal rule: average adjacent utilities, multiply by interval duration and sum.

\[Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{u_i+u_{i+1}}{2}(t_{i+1}-t_i)\]
\[Q=\frac{0.60+0.80}{2}(0.5)+\frac{0.80+0.90}{2}(0.5)=0.775\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Change the visit utilities and the middle assessment month. Observe the two trapezoids and interval calculations.

Death and missing data

Suppose the last utility is 0.80 at month six and the patient dies at month nine. Assuming constant utility until death gives 0.20 QALYs in those three months; linear decline to zero gives 0.10. Choose an assumption justified by the clinical course and test it. Utility after death is zero. A missing questionnaire from a living patient is not zero.

Comparing arms

Estimate participant-level QALYs and compare mean outcomes, accounting for baseline utility imbalance, missing observations and censoring. Regression adjustment for baseline utility helps avoid attributing initial differences to treatment. [4] Restricting analysis to survivors can discard mortality effects.

10. How treatments generate QALYs

Treatment can improve quality of life, extend survival, or do both. A survival improvement does not guarantee a QALY improvement.

Standard care produces five life years at 0.60, or 3.00 QALYs. Five years at 0.80 yields 4.00; seven years at 0.60 yields 4.20; seven years at 0.80 yields 5.60. Six years at 0.60 produces fewer QALYs than five years at 0.80.

\[\Delta Q=u_Bt_B-u_At_A\]

11. Decision trees

A decision tree weights the QALYs for each possible outcome by its probability to calculate expected QALYs per starting patient.

Suppose success occurs in 80% of patients and produces 0.90 QALYs, while failure produces 0.50. Both outcomes apply for the entire one-year horizon in this simplified example.

\[E[Q]=0.8(0.9)+0.2(0.5)=0.82\]

12. Markov models

Weight the proportion occupying each state by the state utility, then multiply by cycle duration in years.

\[Q_k=\Delta t\sum_s p_{s,k}u_s\]

13. Partitioned survival models

The areas under survival curves determine expected time spent in each health state.

Overall survival (OS) gives the proportion alive. Progression-free survival (PFS) gives the proportion alive without progression. Their difference gives the proportion alive after progression.

\[p_{PF}(t)=S_{PFS}(t),\quad p_{PD}(t)=S_{OS}(t)-S_{PFS}(t),\quad p_D(t)=1-S_{OS}(t)\]
\[Q=u_{PF}\int_0^T S_{PFS}(t)\,dt+u_{PD}\int_0^T[S_{OS}(t)-S_{PFS}(t)]\,dt\]

Three expected progression-free years at 0.80 plus two progressed years at 0.50 yield 3.40 QALYs. If a treatment delays progression by one expected year while leaving survival unchanged, that year moves from utility 0.50 to 0.80.

\[\Delta Q=\Delta t_{PF}(u_{PF}-u_{PD})\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Keep OS at five expected years in both arms. Adjust the utility gap and additional progression-free time. The bars show state occupancy time, not survival probabilities.

If the utility gap is zero, delaying progression gives no QALY gain under these assumptions. Progression status must be shown to affect quality of life; toxicity may also alter the result. In an actual model ensure PFS never exceeds OS.

General survival connection

When 60% of starting patients remain alive and their average utility is 0.80, expected utility per starting patient is 0.48 at that time. Accumulate this across the horizon.

\[Q=\int_0^T S(t)E[u(t)\mid\text{alive at }t]\,dt\]

Median survival is not mean survival. Multiplying median survival by utility generally does not estimate QALYs. Explore the dedicated partitioned survival lab.

14. Adverse events, ageing and comorbidities

Adjust utility only for health burdens not already captured by the underlying evidence.

Adverse events

A decrement of 0.20 lasting 14 days causes approximately 0.00767 QALYs lost per event. If 10% experience the event once, the expected loss is 0.000767 QALYs per treated patient.

\[E[Q_{\text{loss}}]=p_{AE}\,d_{AE}\frac{D_{AE}}{365}\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Change event probability, utility decrement and duration. For recurrent events, expected event frequency may be required instead of incidence.

Ageing and comorbidities

Population utility is 0.90 at baseline and 0.75 in the future. Disease utility at baseline is 0.72. Additive adjustment retains the absolute disease decrement; multiplicative adjustment retains the relative decrement.

\[u_{\text{add}}=0.75-(0.90-0.72)=0.57\]
\[u_{\text{mult}}=0.75\frac{0.72}{0.90}=0.60\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Compare the two approaches. Neither is universally correct, especially for overlapping health problems or negative utilities.

Check whether measured utilities already include toxicity, ageing or comorbidities. Avoid double counting. [2]

15. Discounting future QALYs

Discounting expresses future health benefits in present-value terms, not a change in actual patient health.

\[Q_{\text{PV}}=\frac{Q_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

A contribution of 0.80 QALYs assigned to the end of year two at 3.5% per year has present value approximately 0.7468.

16. Cost-utility analysis and decision-making

Cost-utility analysis compares the costs and QALYs of alternatives. A more effective intervention is not necessarily cost-effective.

Standard care has three progression-free and two progressed years; the new treatment has four and 1.5 years respectively. At utilities 0.80 and 0.50, total QALYs are 3.40 and 3.95. Costs are $30,000 and $42,000. These inputs are undiscounted teaching examples.

\[\Delta C=42,000-30,000=12,000,\qquad\Delta Q=3.95-3.40=0.55\]
\[\mathrm{ICER}=\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Q}\approx \$21,818/\text{QALY}\]

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) is the additional cost per additional QALY in the more costly, more effective case. Incremental net monetary benefit (INMB) subtracts incremental cost from the value of incremental health.

\[\mathrm{INMB}=\lambda\Delta Q-\Delta C\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Change costs, QALYs and the illustrative threshold. Explore dominance, equal effectiveness, and the less costly, less effective case.

At a $25,000 threshold, INMB is $1,750. At $20,000 it is −$1,000. Thresholds are illustrative, not national policy. For multiple alternatives, conduct full incremental analysis including extended dominance. A cost-effective intervention can still be unaffordable. QALYs allow comparison across diseases, but policy decisions also consider severity, equity, unmet need and budgets.

17. Uncertainty and methodological pitfalls

The longer patients remain in a state, the more influential its utility is on undiscounted QALYs, holding occupancy constant.

\[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial u_s}=t_s\]
INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Change expected time, central utility and its uncertainty range to inspect the impact on QALYs.

Deterministic sensitivity analysis varies inputs; probabilistic sensitivity analysis samples uncertain parameters jointly; scenario analysis tests evidence and structural choices. Beta distributions cannot directly represent negative utility. Consider correlations for utilities estimated from the same data. [2]

Limitations

Conventional QALYs treat equal QALY gains equally. Two health trajectories with equal utilities and durations in a different order have equal undiscounted QALYs, even when patients prefer one trajectory. Generic instruments may miss important symptoms. Survival gains at lower utilities yield fewer QALYs, raising equity concerns. QALYs are not a measure of intrinsic human worth.

Practical audit checklist

  • Do not divide ordinary QoL scores by 100 and call them utilities.
  • Use comparable instruments and appropriate tariffs.
  • Preserve valid negative utilities; separate death from missing data.
  • Use expected time rather than median survival and convert cycles to years.
  • Do not double count adverse events or age effects.
  • Account for baseline imbalance, missingness, and censoring in trial analysis.
  • Test uncertainty, structural choices and discounting; report life years alongside QALYs.

18. Knowledge checks

Answer before revealing the explanation.

1. EQ-VAS is 70. Is utility necessarily 0.70?

No. Rating-scale anchors differ from QALY anchors.

2. Utility 0.60 for six months: how many QALYs?
\[0.60(6/12)=0.30\]
3. Utility 0.60 at baseline and 0.90 at year one: linear interpolation?
\[(0.60+0.90)/2=0.75\]
4. One year moves from utility 0.50 to 0.80. OS unchanged: QALY gain?
\[1(0.80-0.50)=0.30\]
5. Can longer survival generate fewer QALYs?

Yes, if reduced quality of life outweighs longer survival.

6. Does missing utility mean zero?

No. Death is assigned zero; missing health data require appropriate handling.

7. A treatment saves money but loses QALYs. Does a positive ICER prove cost-effectiveness?

No. The ratio describes savings per QALY forgone. Compare INMB at the threshold.

8. Does a 0.20 decrement for two weeks mean losing 0.20 QALYs?

No, weight by duration. The loss is approximately 0.00767 QALYs per event.
