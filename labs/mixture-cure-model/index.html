Lab 01 · Survival extrapolation

Mixture Cure Models

A mixture cure model asks whether the trial population is better represented as two latent groups: a proportion whose disease-related excess mortality eventually disappears, and a proportion who remain subject to the disease-related survival process.

Central modelling question: is there credible evidence for a statistically cured fraction, or are we simply forcing an attractive long-term plateau into immature survival data?
1 · What is it?

The model separates cure from survival among patients who are not cured

In a mixture cure model (MCM), the population is represented as a mixture of two unobserved, or latent, groups. The model estimates the probability of belonging to the statistically cured group and separately models survival for the uncured group.

S(t) = π × Scured(t) + (1 − π) × Suncured(t)

π is the cure fraction. In many HTA applications, cured patients are assumed eventually to experience mortality close to an age- and sex-matched general population. The uncured subgroup follows a selected survival distribution. The two subgroup curves are then combined to obtain survival for the whole treatment population.

Statistical cure is not necessarily biological eradication of disease. It means the excess mortality attributable to the disease is assumed to have disappeared, so long-term mortality approaches an appropriate background rate.

What the cure fraction changes

  • The weight placed on long-term background survival.
  • The shape of the population hazard over time.
  • Mean survival and therefore lifetime QALYs and costs.
  • Potentially the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio when cure differs by treatment.

What it does not mean

  • A visible KM plateau automatically proves cure.
  • Everyone still alive at the end of trial follow-up is cured.
  • The cure fraction can be reliably estimated from any short trial.
  • An MCM should be selected solely because it has the best AIC or visual fit.
2 · When to use it?

Start with the clinical mechanism and long-term hazard, not the model name

Evidence that supports exploring an MCM

  • A clinically credible mechanism for durable remission or long-term disease control.
  • A survival curve or hazard pattern consistent with a subgroup becoming long-term survivors.
  • Sufficient follow-up and patients remaining at risk to inform the tail.
  • External evidence, registries, later trial cuts, or expert judgement supporting convergence towards background mortality.
  • A disease setting where long-term remission is already well established.

Warning signs

  • Immature follow-up with no emerging plateau.
  • Very few patients remaining at risk in the tail.
  • The cure fraction is identified mainly by distributional assumptions rather than data.
  • Background mortality is implausible for long-term survivors because important excess mortality remains.
  • The cure assumption is introduced only because conventional models extrapolate poorly.

Published examples

Advanced melanomaBullement et al. retrospectively compared extrapolations against longer-term follow-up. A mixture-cure model was among the approaches that reproduced 5-year survival reasonably well, while several standard parametric approaches underestimated it.

4 · Guidance and methodological evidence

How to justify the method in HTA

SourcePractical implication for an analyst
NICE DSU TSD 21
Flexible methods for survival analysis		TSD 21 treats cure models as one option for complex survival and extrapolation. The key issue is not merely in-sample fit. Analysts should investigate the observed hazard, justify the structural assumptions, assess extrapolated survival and hazards, and use external information where it can improve long-term plausibility.
Latimer & Rutherford, 2024
PharmacoEconomics tutorial		Cure models are most useful when a cure assumption is clinically defensible. The paper emphasises understanding whether cure is identifiable from available follow-up, carefully specifying background mortality, checking model behaviour, and comparing mixture and non-mixture formulations rather than treating cure modelling as a mechanical fit exercise.
ISPOR / Value in Health evidenceISPOR-published methodological case studies show why validation against later data cuts and external evidence matters. In several immuno-oncology examples, models incorporating survival heterogeneity, including MCMs, improved medium-term predictions. This is evidence supporting evaluation and validation, not an official ISPOR mandate to use MCMs.
Decision rule: do not ask “Does the MCM fit the KM curve?” first. Ask whether statistical cure is clinically plausible, whether the available data can identify it, what long-term mortality should be for cured patients, and whether external evidence supports the extrapolation. Only then compare candidate model forms.
