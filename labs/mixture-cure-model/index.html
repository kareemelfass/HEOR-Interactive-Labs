Lab 01 · Survival extrapolation

Mixture Cure Models

Understand why cure models are different from standard survival models, what each component means, and how the model combines estimated cure probability, uncured survival, and expected mortality into a population survival curve.

Central question: does the evidence support a statistically cured subgroup whose excess disease-related mortality eventually disappears, or is an apparent long-term plateau simply a consequence of immature follow-up?
1 · Theoretical background

Why mixture cure models exist

Standard parametric survival models generally use one distribution to represent the survival experience of the full population. This can be restrictive when the population is heterogeneous and a subgroup has markedly different long-term survival. NICE DSU TSD 21 discusses cure models as one option for representing complex long-term survival behaviour when a cure assumption is clinically credible.

S(t) = π × Scured(t) + (1 − π) × Suncured(t)

A mixture cure model explicitly represents two latent, meaning unobserved, subgroups. One subgroup is statistically cured and eventually loses the excess mortality associated with the disease. The other remains subject to the disease-related survival process.

Statistical cure is not necessarily biological eradication of disease. It is a population-level survival concept. The excess hazard associated with the disease is assumed eventually to disappear for the cured group.

How it differs from other survival models

ModelPopulation structureLong-term behaviour

Important: a plateau in the Kaplan-Meier curve is not by itself proof of cure. TSD 21 emphasises clinical plausibility, information in the tail, hazard behaviour, and external evidence when assessing extrapolations.
2 · Terms and model components

What each part of the model means

Cure fraction, πThe model-estimated proportion belonging to the cured latent class. It is not simply the proportion alive at the end of the trial.
Latent classA subgroup whose membership is not directly observed for each patient and is inferred by the statistical model.
Uncured survival, Su(t)The survival function for patients who remain subject to the disease-related survival process.
Cured survival, Sc(t)The survival experience assigned to cured patients. In HEOR this is often linked to expected general-population mortality.
Background mortalityExpected mortality unrelated to the excess disease process, commonly derived from age-, sex-, calendar-year-, and country-specific life tables.
Excess mortalityMortality above the expected background rate that is attributable to the disease process.
Cure timeThe point at which excess disease-related mortality is assumed to have disappeared. This is different from the cure fraction.
Mixture survivalThe population survival curve obtained by weighting cured and uncured survival according to the estimated cure probability.
3 · Published examples

Where cure modelling has been applied

Advanced melanomaBullement et al. retrospectively compared several extrapolation approaches against longer-term follow-up. A mixture cure model was among the approaches that reproduced 5-year survival reasonably well.
Value in Health, 2019
CheckMate 067, advanced melanomaModels explicitly representing long-term survival heterogeneity, including mixture cure approaches, were compared across successive data cuts to assess extrapolation stability.
Value in Health, 2022
DLBCL and CAR-TCure modelling has been considered where long-term remission after CAR-T therapy could plausibly result in survival approaching the matched general population.
NICE technology appraisal evidence and later methodological discussions
Immune-oncology settingsLong-term responder subgroups have motivated cure and related heterogeneity models in several oncology economic evaluations and validation studies.
HTA and health-economic literature
Published success does not create a general rule. Cure models can overestimate survival when cure is absent or follow-up is too immature. The structural assumption must be justified independently of statistical fit.
4 · Worked example and simulation

Build the model from its three components

Example specification

  • Cure model: logistic regression
  • Uncured survival: Weibull distribution
  • Cured survival: age-dependent simulated life-table mortality
  • Time horizon: 30 years

What to expect before changing anything

  • Increasing cure probability should mainly lift the long-term tail.
  • Improving uncured survival should mainly affect early and intermediate survival.
  • Changing Weibull shape should alter the trajectory of the uncured hazard.
  • Increasing starting age should pull the cured curve downward through higher expected mortality.

The cure fraction is not entered directly. It is generated from a cure submodel. Here:

logit(π) = β₀ + β₁ × Treatment

The population curve is then:

How the point on the mixture curve is constructed

Expected parameter behaviour

Increase β₀ or β₁

Estimated cure probability rises. More weight is assigned to cured survival, lifting the long-term tail.

Increase median uncured survival

The uncured curve moves upward, mainly changing early and intermediate survival.

Change Weibull shape

The hazard trajectory changes. k > 1 means increasing hazard, k = 1 constant hazard, and k < 1 decreasing hazard.

Increase starting age

Expected mortality rises, so cured survival declines more quickly and the population curve does not form an unrealistic permanent plateau.

5 · Guidance and methodological evidence

How to justify the model in HTA

SourcePractical implication
NICE DSU TSD 21Cure models are one option for complex extrapolation. Analysts should examine the observed hazard, justify the structural assumption, assess extrapolated hazards and survival, and use external evidence where appropriate. Within-trial fit alone is insufficient.
Latimer & Rutherford, 2024Assess whether cure is clinically plausible and identifiable from the available follow-up, specify expected mortality carefully, and compare alternative cure formulations and non-cure models.
ISPOR / Value in Health methodological evidenceValidation studies show the value of checking predictions against later data cuts and external evidence. These studies support evaluation and validation, but do not constitute an official ISPOR requirement to use mixture cure models.
