1The speedometer idea

Picture a car trip. The speedometer shows how fast you are going right now. The odometer shows how far you have gone in total. If someone asks for your average speed, you divide the distance on the odometer by the time on the clock.

Survival data works the same way. Instead of kilometres, you collect risk.

2Three linked curves

Before averaging anything, we need the three curves that describe a survival experience, and how they connect.

  • Hazard \(h(t)\) is the event rate at time \(t\), counted only among people who are still event-free. Its unit is events per unit of time, such as per person-year. It is a rate, so it is not a probability.
  • Cumulative hazard \(H(t)\) is the area under the hazard curve from 0 to \(t\): \[ H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du \] It only ever goes up.
  • Survival \(S(t)\) is the chance of still being event-free at time \(t\).

The link between them is the key identity of this whole tutorial:

\[ S(t)=e^{-H(t)} \qquad\text{which means}\qquad H(t)=-\ln S(t) \]

Tiny example. Suppose the hazard stays at 0.10 per year for 5 years. The area under a flat line is height times width, so \(H(5)=0.10\times 5=0.50\). Then \(S(5)=e^{-0.5}=0.607\). About 61 in 100 people are still event-free at year 5.

3What average hazard means

The hazard usually changes over time. A single average gives you one number to report, to compare, or to feed into a model. Just like average speed, you take the total collected (the cumulative hazard) and divide by the length of the window.

\[ \text{average hazard over }[a,b]=\frac{H(b)-H(a)}{b-a} \]
  • When the window starts at zero, it becomes \(H(t)/t\), which equals \(-\ln S(t)/t\).
  • In words: it is the constant hazard that would collect the same total risk over the same window.
  • On a picture, the area under the real curve equals the area of a flat rectangle at the average height. That is the dashed box in the tool below.

The four numbers the tool reports

The tool below shows four readouts for the window you choose. Here is what each one means, using one small story: 100 people are event-free at the start of a 5-year window. By the end, 40 have had the event and 60 have not. Together they spend about 391 years at risk.

1. Cumulative hazard in window

\( H(b)-H(a)=\text{area under the hazard curve between } a \text{ and } b \)

Meaning: the total risk collected during the window. It is the odometer reading for just that stretch of the trip. It has no unit of its own, because it is a rate multiplied by time.

In the story: \(-\ln(60/100)=0.511\).

2. Time-average hazard (per year)

\( \dfrac{\text{cumulative hazard in window}}{\text{window length}}=\dfrac{H(b)-H(a)}{b-a} \)

Meaning: the single steady hazard that would collect the same total risk in the same time. This is the average speed. Every moment of the window counts equally.

In the story: \(0.511/5=0.102\) per year.

3. Person-time weighted (per year)

\( \dfrac{\text{events in window}}{\text{person-years at risk in window}} \)

Meaning: the ordinary incidence rate. Moments count more when more people are still at risk, because those moments contribute more person-years. It is the version used in the survival-weighted average hazard of section 6.

In the story: \(40/391=0.102\) per year.

4. Chance of staying event-free through the window

\( \dfrac{S(b)}{S(a)}=e^{-\left(H(b)-H(a)\right)} \)

Meaning: for people still event-free at the start of the window, the probability they are still event-free at its end. It is a probability, so it runs from 0% to 100%, unlike the two rates above.

In the story: \(e^{-0.511}=0.60\), so 60%.

  • How they connect. Number 2 is number 1 divided by the window length. Number 4 is number 1 turned into a probability with \(e^{-x}\). Number 3 needs the survival curve, because it counts person-years.
  • Why 2 and 3 match in the story. The hazard in this story is steady. When the hazard changes over time, the two split apart, and the tool shows by how much.

What to notice

  • With steady risk, both averages equal the hazard itself. Nothing is hidden.
  • With early spike, then falls, the average sits well above the late-year hazard and well below the early peak. One number cannot tell you the shape.
  • Move the window start to 1. The average drops sharply, because the spike now sits outside the window. The window changes the answer, so always report it.

4Calculating it from real data

With real patients you do not know the curve. You estimate the cumulative hazard from the observed events, then divide by the window length. The simplest estimator is the Nelson-Aalen estimator (the standard step-by-step way to estimate cumulative hazard).

  1. Choose the window \([a,b]\), for example years 1 to 10.
  2. List each event time in the window. At each one, note \(d\) (events) and \(n\) (people still at risk just before).
  3. Add the steps: each event time contributes \(d/n\) to the cumulative hazard.
  4. Divide the sum by the window length \(b-a\).
  5. Standard error: divide the standard error of the summed cumulative hazard by the same length. The length is a fixed number, so it scales the estimate and its error equally.
\[ \text{average hazard}=\frac{1}{b-a}\sum_{a\lt t_i\le b}\frac{d_i}{n_i} \]

This is the method in Bull and Spiegelhalter (1997, Section 6.2, p. 1057). Their Table VI reports an average hazard of 0.043 per year for years 1 to 10 in one group, built from steps of 0.077, 0.111 and 0.200 over a 9-year window:

\[ \frac{0.077+0.111+0.200}{9}=0.0431 \]

If you already have a survival curve, use the matching version:

\[ \text{average hazard}=\frac{-\ln\!\left[\dfrac{S(b)}{S(a)}\right]}{b-a} \]

The two routes estimate the same thing but are not identical in small samples, because \(-\ln\) of a Kaplan-Meier estimate is always a little larger than the Nelson-Aalen sum. The tool below shows the gap.

The same thing in R

library(survival)

fit_km <- survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ 1, data = d)
fit_na <- survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ 1, data = d,
                  stype = 2, ctype = 1)

avg_haz <- function(fit, a, b) {
  s <- summary(fit, times = c(a, b), extend = TRUE)$surv
  -log(s[2] / s[1]) / (b - a)
}

avg_haz(fit_na, 1, 10)
avg_haz(fit_km, 1, 10)
  • library(survival) loads the standard survival package.
  • fit_km fits the Kaplan-Meier curve. Surv(time, status) bundles follow-up time with the event flag, and ~ 1 means one curve for everyone.
  • fit_na asks for a curve built from the Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard: ctype = 1 picks that estimator and stype = 2 sets survival to \(e^{-H}\).
  • avg_haz reads survival at the window start and end, then applies \(-\ln\!\left[S(b)/S(a)\right]/(b-a)\). extend = TRUE keeps the call from failing if \(b\) is past the last follow-up time.
  • The last two lines give the Nelson-Aalen and Kaplan-Meier versions for years 1 to 10.

5Reading the number

Take the average hazard of 0.043 per year over years 1 to 10. Here is how to say it correctly and what to avoid.

  • Say this: "Over years 1 to 10, the hazard behaved on average like a constant 0.043 per year." It collected the same total risk as the real, changing hazard.
  • It is a rate, not a risk. 0.043 per year does not mean a 4.3% chance of the event. For people still event-free at year 1, the chance of an event by year 10 is \[ 1-e^{-0.043\times 9}=1-e^{-0.388}\approx 0.32 \] which is about 32%.
  • It hides the shape. A hazard that spikes then fades and a hazard that rises slowly can share the same average. Plot the hazard too.
  • Windows give a story. In the same paper, the average hazard in the first year was 0.237, against 0.043 for years 1 to 10. Two windows show a steep early fall that one overall average would bury.

6Two averages that look alike

"Average hazard" can mean two slightly different things. Both are valid. They answer different questions, so they should not be swapped without saying so.

Time averagePerson-time weighted average
IdeaEvery moment counts equallyMoments count more when more people are still at risk
Formula\(H(b)-H(a)\), divided by window lengthEvents in the window divided by person-time in the window
Also known asAverage hazard (Bull and Spiegelhalter)Incidence rate; average hazard with survival weight, AHSW (Uno and Horiguchi)
From the start, up to \(\tau\)\(\dfrac{-\ln S(\tau)}{\tau}\)\(\dfrac{1-S(\tau)}{\mathrm{RMST}(\tau)}\)

RMST is the restricted mean survival time, the area under the survival curve up to \(\tau\):

\[ \mathrm{RMST}(\tau)=\int_0^{\tau} S(t)\,dt \]

It is the expected number of event-free person-years per person in that window.

  • With a constant hazard, both give the same number.
  • With a falling hazard, the person-time weighted version is higher, because the high early hazard happens while most people are still at risk. With a rising hazard it is lower. You can see this in the tool in section 3.
  • The weighted version can be estimated from a Kaplan-Meier curve with censored data, and Uno and Horiguchi built tests and a regression model on it. It is available in the R package survAH.

Do not confuse either with the "average hazard ratio". That is a different object: a single hazard ratio that summarises a ratio changing over time, estimated for example by weighted Cox regression (Kalbfleisch and Prentice 1981; Schemper, Wakounig and Heinze 2009).

7Where it is used

In biostatistics

  • Describing change over time. Report the average hazard in separate windows, as in the first year versus years 1 to 10 above.
  • Comparing groups. Divide one group's average hazard by the other's over the same window. In Bull and Spiegelhalter's example the ratio was 3.59 in the first year and 1.49 over years 1 to 10, so the relative difference shrank over time.
  • When hazards are not proportional. A Cox hazard ratio assumes a constant ratio. If curves cross or an effect is delayed, the ratio and difference of weighted average hazards, or a weighted Cox model, give a summary that stays meaningful.

In health economics (HEOR)

  • Turning data into model inputs. A cycle length in a Markov model rarely matches the follow-up of a study, so a probability must be converted to a rate and back.
  • Checking extrapolations. Compare the average hazard in the observed window with the average hazard the fitted curve implies beyond it. A sudden jump or fall is a warning sign to investigate.
  • Exponential models. A constant hazard is simply events divided by total person-time.
  • Summarising waning or delayed effects in a way that decision makers can read.

Worked example: from a 5-year risk to a cycle probability

A study reports that 40% of patients had the event within 5 years, so \(S(5)=0.60\). Your model runs in 1-year and 3-month cycles.

  1. Average hazard: \(-\ln(0.60)/5=0.5108/5=0.1022\) per year.
  2. 1-year probability: \(1-e^{-0.1022\times 1}=0.0971\), about 9.7%.
  3. 3-month probability: \(1-e^{-0.1022\times 0.25}=0.0252\), about 2.5%.

The general rule behind steps 2 and 3, for a cycle of length \(c\) years:

\[ r=\frac{-\ln(1-p)}{t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad p_c=1-e^{-r\,c} \]

The tempting shortcut, 40% divided by 5 years, gives 8% per year. That is too low, because risk is collected on a shrinking group of people who are still event-free. All of these steps assume the hazard is roughly constant inside the window, so check that before you trust them.

8Common mistakes

  1. Treating a rate as a probability. A hazard of 0.1 per year is not a 10% risk.
  2. Dividing a probability by time. Use \(-\ln(1-p)/t\) to get the rate.
  3. Forgetting the window. The same data give different averages for different windows.
  4. Averaging yearly hazards without weights. If windows differ in length, weight each by its length, or add the cumulative hazards and divide by total time.
  5. Using \(-\ln S(t)/t\) for a window that does not start at zero. Use the ratio \(S(b)/S(a)\).
  6. Mixing up the two averages or confusing either with the average hazard ratio.
  7. Assuming the hazard really is constant because you reported one average.
