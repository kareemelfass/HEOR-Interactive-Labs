RMST and AFT, demystified

One cancer survival dataset. Two ways of describing benefit. Build the Kaplan–Meier curve, count survival months, stretch the survival clock, and translate the results into life-years and QALYs.

No prior modelling requiredFully worked calculationsLive survival curvesPublished HEOR examples
Learning note: All patient data and adjustable scenario outputs on this page are illustrative, not clinical trial evidence. Four fully observed death times per arm are used so every calculation is visible. Published studies are clearly labelled in the evidence section.
First, the distinction

Both talk about time, but answer different questions

RMST: accumulated time

By month 24, how many months of life did the average patient accumulate? RMST adds the area underneath a survival curve, stopping at a chosen time.

Measured in months
AFT: a stretched clock

How much longer does it take for events to occur under the new treatment? The accelerated failure time model multiplies the distribution of event times by a common factor.

Measured as a time ratio
The aim of this lab: See why the same example can produce 3.75 months of RMST gain at month 24 and an AFT time ratio of 1.50, without any contradiction.

Chapter 1 continued · Build the Kaplan–Meier curves

Each death reduces survival by one quarter

With four patients at baseline and no censoring, the Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve starts at 100%, then drops to 75%, 50%, 25% and 0% as each death occurs. The height remains constant between deaths. The shaded area under each curve until the vertical gold line is its RMST.

Standard treatmentNew treatmentRMST stopping time
Why does each event produce a 25 percentage-point drop here?

Before the first death, all 4 are at risk, so the conditional survival factor is 3/4 and KM falls to 0.75. Before the second death, 3 remain at risk, so the factor is 2/3. The cumulative survival becomes (3/4) × (2/3) = 0.50. The two remaining deaths bring the curve to 0.25 and then 0. In real trials, censoring changes the risk sets and therefore the size of the subsequent KM drops.

Chapter 2 · Calculate RMST, one small rectangle at a time

RMST counts the survival months under the curve

At any time interval, multiply how long the interval lasts by the proportion still alive during it. Sum all those rectangular areas until τ. That is the restricted mean survival time.

\[ \operatorname{RMST}(\tau)=\int_0^\tau S(t)\,dt = E\{\min(T,\tau)\} \]

Step A. Follow the survival-curve rectangles

An event at the beginning of an interval changes the survival probability for the following interval. Every row below shows the complete multiplication, not just the final answer.

Step B. Verify with the observed survival times

There is no censoring here. Therefore, we can also cap each observed death time at τ, add them and divide by four. This gives exactly the same answer as the KM area.

What changes with real censored data? You cannot generally average only the observed event times. Use KM estimation and integrate its survival curve up to a horizon supported by follow-up. Patients censored before τ are handled through the KM risk sets. This demo has no confidence intervals, because its four illustrative outcomes are not intended for inference.
Chapter 3 · The accelerated failure time idea

AFT stretches or compresses the entire survival clock

Return to the same event-time data. Instead of asking how many months have accumulated by τ, ask how the new arm's event-time distribution is transformed from the standard arm's distribution.

\[T_{\mathrm{new}}\ \overset{d}{=}\ \mathrm{TR}\,T_{\mathrm{standard}},\qquad \mathrm{TR}=e^{\beta}\]

The symbol \(\overset{d}{=}\) means equality in distribution. It does not describe a known treatment gain for any specific patient.

Interpret the ratio

The new treatment stretches survival times by 50%.

Ratio above 1: time is longer. Ratio below 1: time is shorter. Ratio 1: no time scaling.

Regression coefficient and survival function

The relation Snew(t) = Sstandard(t / TR) makes the same horizontal stretching visible in the KM chart.
Does an AFT time ratio of 1.50 mean the hazard ratio is 0.50?

No. A time ratio describes the horizontal scaling of event times. A hazard ratio compares instantaneous event rates. Their relationship depends on the chosen survival distribution. Under a Weibull AFT model, the corresponding constant hazard ratio also depends on the Weibull shape parameter. With log-normal or log-logistic AFT models, proportional hazards generally do not hold.

Can we conclude from four observations that the AFT assumption holds?

No. We deliberately constructed the new arm as a 1.5-fold rescaling of the standard arm. In an actual dataset, the AFT regression coefficient is estimated and a distributional assumption must be examined. Inspect model fit, log-time residuals, quantile behaviour and external plausibility. A violation of proportional hazards does not establish an AFT model as correct.

Where does AFT enter an economic model?

1. Fit observed survival

Use observed PFS or OS with an appropriate parametric AFT model. Assess fit against KM curves and diagnose structural assumptions.

2. Project beyond the trial

Compute predicted S(t) over the decision horizon. The assumed distribution and treatment effect determine the long-term tail.

3. Accumulate model outcomes

Integrate projected survival for life-years. Combine survival or health-state occupancy with utilities and costs for QALYs and ICERs.

HEOR warning: A good RMST match inside the trial does not validate an AFT extrapolation beyond follow-up. Two distributions can have similar observed areas but radically different long-term tails.
Chapter 6 · Verify with actual published evidence

How researchers have used RMST and AFT in economic evaluations

These are published results, not outputs of this lab. Each study illustrates a distinct use: measuring incremental survival, checking curve fit, or constructing extrapolated survival models.

RMST in a trial-based cost-effectiveness analysis · 2016

Lueza et al. | Radiotherapy in locally advanced non-small-cell lung cancer

The investigators used individual patient data from 10 trials with roughly 2,000 patients. Depending on how trial-specific information was pooled, estimated RMST differences ranged from 1.7 to 2.5 months. Corresponding mean ICERs ranged from €24,299 to €34,934 per life-year gained; the probability of cost-effectiveness at €25,000 per life-year ranged from 31% to 68%.

HEOR implication: Estimating RMST differences is consequential for accumulated life-years and cost-effectiveness conclusions. The variation was across alternative pooling/estimation strategies, not a time-ratio experiment.

Lueza et al. (2016), PLOS ONE: primary article ↗
RMST as a fit check and AFT for extrapolation · 2016

Amdahl et al. | Pazopanib versus sunitinib in metastatic renal cell carcinoma, Canada

The base-case partitioned-survival model used empirical KM curves from COMPARZ over the observed period. For analyses beyond follow-up, the investigators fitted parametric distributions by AFT regression and assessed them using visual comparisons, information criteria and RMST agreement with empirical KM. A gamma model fitted well but was not used because its tails risked overstating survival.

At list prices, the published base case reported CA$10,293 lower costs and 0.059 additional QALYs with pazopanib compared with sunitinib. These results came from the complete economic model, not simply from an AFT time ratio.

HEOR implication: RMST can assess fit within observed follow-up while AFT supports extrapolation. Neither the RMST fit nor a low AIC can alone justify an implausible long-term tail.

Amdahl et al. (2016), Current Oncology: primary article ↗
AFT models supplying health-economic transition probabilities · 2025

Brinkmann et al. | ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, Germany

Published VISION and TheraP PFS/OS curves were digitised and reconstructed. The investigators fitted parametric accelerated failure time hazard models to generate time-dependent transition probabilities for cohort state-transition models. The third-line comparison of ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 plus standard care versus standard care alone reported €27,200 incremental costs, 0.39 incremental QALYs, and a published €69,418 per QALY ICER.

HEOR implication: Fitted time-to-event distributions feed model transitions, occupancy, costs and QALYs. These economic results depend on the entire model, not only the AFT specification.

Brinkmann et al. (2025), European Journal of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging: primary article ↗
