Decision Tree Basics Lab

Learn how a health-economic decision tree is built, how branch probabilities become pathway probabilities, and how expected costs and QALYs lead to incremental cost-effectiveness analysis.

1. What problem does a decision tree solve?

A decision tree is useful when a decision is followed by a finite sequence of clinically meaningful events over a relatively short time horizon. It makes every possible pathway explicit and attaches probabilities, costs, and health outcomes to those pathways.

Core idea: each complete path represents one possible patient journey. The model combines those journeys using probability-weighted averages.
Square: decision node, representing alternatives under the modeller's control.
Circle: chance node, representing uncertainty.
\[E(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_iX_i\]

This is the mathematical engine of the tree: multiply each consequence by the probability of reaching it, then add.

2. Branch probabilities

Branches leaving a chance node must represent mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive outcomes, so their probabilities sum to 1.

Success
0.70
Failure
0.30
\[P(\mathrm{failure}\mid A)=1-P(\mathrm{success}\mid A)\]

Path probabilities

If a path contains more than one chance event, multiply conditional probabilities along that path.

\[P(\mathrm{success\ and\ AE})=P(\mathrm{success})\times P(\mathrm{AE}\mid\mathrm{success})\]
\[0.70\times0.20=0.14\]
Rule: multiply along a path. Add across mutually exclusive terminal pathways.

Applied published example

The uploaded Marty et al. decision tree compared two long-acting somatostatin receptor ligand injection devices. It branched by administration setting and then by successful injection versus clogging. If clogging occurred, the first dose was assumed wasted and another injection was performed.

The clinical content differs from our teaching example, but the expected-value calculation is the same:

\[E(C)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(\mathrm{path}_j)\,C(\mathrm{path}_j)\]

This is a useful applied cost comparison. It is not a generic cost-utility example, which is why this lab uses a separate fictional example to teach QALYs and the ICER.

5. Exercise: calculate it yourself

Treatment C has treatment cost £4,000, success probability 0.80, cost after success £1,000, cost after failure £5,000, success QALYs 0.90, and failure QALYs 0.40.

\[P(\mathrm{failure})=1-0.80=0.20\]
\[\begin{aligned}E(C_C)&=4000+(0.80\times1000)+(0.20\times5000)\\&=4000+800+1000\\&=5800\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}E(Q_C)&=(0.80\times0.90)+(0.20\times0.40)\\&=0.72+0.08\\&=0.80\end{aligned}\]

Final modelling challenge

A diagnostic strategy can produce a positive or negative test result. Positive patients receive treatment and may respond or not respond. Negative patients receive standard management.

  1. Draw the decision node and alternatives.
  2. Add the test-result chance node.
  3. Add response and non-response branches.
  4. Assign branch probabilities.
  5. Multiply probabilities along each complete path.
  6. Attach costs and QALYs to terminal nodes.
  7. Calculate expected costs and expected QALYs.
  8. Compare incrementally with standard care.
When a tree is not enough: recurrent events, long-term state transitions, and strong time dependence can make a simple tree unwieldy. Those situations may call for Markov, semi-Markov, multi-state, or other state-transition approaches.
