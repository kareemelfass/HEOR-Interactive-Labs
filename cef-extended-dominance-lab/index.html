Extended dominance & the cost-effectiveness frontier

Understand why an apparently cost-effective treatment can be inefficient, then build and export your own frontier using up to eleven strategies (one index treatment and ten comparators).

1. Start with the decision problem

We want to maximise expected health gains for a given value of resources. Comparing each intervention with standard care is not sufficient when several mutually exclusive strategies are available. We need to compare all relevant alternatives incrementally.

2. Simple dominance

C produces more QALYs at a lower cost than D. No trade-off is needed. Remove D from the sequential ICER calculations. Equal cost with better health, or equal health at lower cost, also qualifies.

3. Extended dominance: the intermediate option is inefficient

B costs more than A and produces more health. C costs more than B and produces more health. Neither individually dominates B. But the incremental cost per additional QALY falls as we move from B to C.

\[ICER_{B:A}=\frac{30,000-10,000}{3-2}=20,000\qquad ICER_{C:B}=\frac{40,000-30,000}{4-3}=10,000\]

Imagine 100 comparable patients. Treating everyone with B produces 300 QALYs and costs $3 million. Treating 50 with A and 50 with C produces the same 300 QALYs for $2.5 million. That is a saving of $500,000. This is a population allocation, not combination therapy for one patient.

For any intermediate effect, the proportion receiving the more effective option is \(w=(E_B-E_A)/(E_C-E_A)\). The cost of that allocation is \(C_{mix}=(1-w)C_A+wC_C\). B is extendedly dominated when its cost exceeds this value. This assumes comparable eligible patients and linear aggregation of expected costs and outcomes.

4. The frontier is the lower convex boundary

Plot QALYs horizontally and costs vertically. The frontier connects efficient options. B sits above the line from A to C. D is simply dominated. Once B is removed, the final ICER is \(ICER_{C:A}=30,000/2=15,000\) per QALY. Every time you remove an option, recalculate the adjacent ICERs. The slopes along the final frontier must be non-decreasing.

5. The threshold selects an option, not the frontier

Net monetary benefit (NMB) converts health into monetary units at threshold \(\lambda\): \(NMB_i=\lambda E_i-C_i\). At $15,000/QALY A and C tie; below it A wins and above it C wins. B never maximises NMB. Collinear strategies may tie at exactly one threshold; the plotter retains them as frontier points. Exact duplicates are reported separately.

Try it: Load the tutorial example below, choose incremental coordinates relative to B, and change the threshold. The origin moves but the frontier and the NMB winner do not change. A negative incremental ICER alone is not a decision rule.

6. Absolute and incremental planes

Absolute coordinates show each strategy's total expected costs and QALYs. Incremental coordinates subtract the selected reference strategy from every point. The four quadrants describe pairwise differences relative to that reference; they do not determine the overall frontier.

On the incremental plane, the line through the origin with slope λ indicates equal NMB to the reference. A line through the NMB-optimal strategy instead shows the best achievable NMB. Points below the reference line have higher NMB than the reference, but may still lose to another option.

Interpretation and limits

Use the full set of relevant, mutually exclusive strategies. The analysis is deterministic and assumes a common comparable population; subgroup differences, feasibility and parameter uncertainty may change conclusions. In PSA, evaluate NMB for all strategies in each draw rather than removing base-case dominated strategies permanently. The CEF is not a cost-effectiveness acceptability frontier (CEAF), which is an uncertainty-based probability display.
