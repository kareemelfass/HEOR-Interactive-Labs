Mixture Cure Models, explained from the ground up

What they are, when to use them, what data they need, a full worked calculation, real published examples, the traps, and how they are judged in HTA.

In one minute
  • A mixture cure model (MCM) says your patients are two hidden groups: cured patients who will never have the event, and uncured patients who will have it sooner or later.
  • The curve is S(t) = π + (1 − π) × Su(t). It levels off at π (the cure fraction) instead of sliding to zero.
  • The model never labels a patient as cured. It estimates a share (π) and, for each survivor, a probability of being cured.
  • There is no cure date inside the model. A "cure point" is something you derive afterwards from a threshold you choose.
  • Use it only when cure is biologically believable, the Kaplan-Meier curve really flattens, and follow-up is long enough to prove it.
  • The cure fraction is the hardest number to estimate. In the simulation in section 8 it was unreliable until follow-up was about 3.5 times the median time to event of the uncured.
  • In HTA, an MCM is judged mostly on one question: can you defend π with outside evidence?

Throughout this page, cured is always teal and uncured is always amber. Every technical term is explained the first time it appears, and there is a short glossary at the end.

1 · The idea

Two hidden groups, one curve

The problem a standard model cannot solve

A survival curve S(t) shows the share of patients still event-free at time t (the event can be death, relapse, progression). A Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve is the step-shaped estimate of that curve built from trial data, where patients who leave the study without an event are censored (we only know they were event-free up to their last visit).

A standard model such as Weibull or log-logistic gives every patient some risk at every moment. So the curve keeps sliding down toward zero, even when the real KM curve has gone flat. After some treatments (CAR-T cells, some immunotherapies, curative surgery) a flat tail is exactly what we see.

The mixture idea, with a picture in your head

  • Picture 1,000 patients walking down a corridor.
  • 450 carry an invisible "never relapse" ticket. They keep walking forever.
  • The other 550 each carry a hidden countdown clock. When a clock hits zero, that patient leaves (relapses).
  • Nobody can see the tickets. We only watch who is still in the corridor.
  • As time passes, countdown patients leave, so the people still walking are more and more likely to be ticket holders. The curve flattens because the corridor is filling up with the cured, not because anyone flipped a switch.

The formula, read in plain words

S(t) = π + (1 − π) × S_u(t)
  • π (pi) is the cure fraction: the share of patients who will never have the event from this disease. It is a number between 0 and 1.
  • Su(t) is the survival curve of the uncured group only. It is called the latency distribution (latency means "the waiting time before the event"). It is a normal parametric curve such as Weibull or log-logistic.
  • Cured patients contribute π at every time, because they never leave.
  • Uncured patients contribute (1 − π) × Su(t), which shrinks toward 0 over time.
  • So when t gets large, Su(t) → 0 and S(t) → π. The curve has a floor.

Three more formulas you will use

Hazard: h(t) = (1 − π) × f_u(t) / S(t) Chance a survivor is cured: P(cured | alive at t) = π / S(t) With background mortality: S_o(t) = S_b(t) × [ π + (1 − π) × S_u(t) ]
  • The hazard h(t) is the instantaneous risk of the event at time t, among people still event-free. fu(t) is the density of the uncured group (how fast they are leaving at time t).
  • The hazard falls on its own as S(t) falls toward the floor π, because the denominator S(t) stops shrinking. That is the "smooth transition" with no hard switch.
  • P(cured | alive at t) reads "the probability of being cured, given you are still alive and event-free at t". The numerator π never changes. The denominator S(t) shrinks. So the probability rises.
  • Background mortality Sb(t) is the survival of the general population of the same age and sex, taken from a life table (a published table of death rates by age). Cured patients are free of this disease, but they can still die of other causes. The third formula follows Felizzi et al. (2021).
Key point

A specific patient cannot be labelled cured or uncured. "Cured" describes a share of the population. What the model gives you is a probability for each survivor, which is why it never needs a "cure date". Sections 3 and 4 explain this in detail.

Latency curve used in this tutorial

For the examples, the uncured follow a log-logistic latency:

S_u(t) = 1 / (1 + λ × t^γ)

Here λ is a scale-type parameter and γ is the shape.

Software warning

In R's flexsurv package the log-logistic is written S(t) = 1 / (1 + (t / scale)shape), so λ = scale−shape. Always convert before copying parameters between tools.

Mixture versus non-mixture

  • Mixture (above): the cure fraction π is a direct parameter. This is the form most HTA submissions use.
  • Non-mixture or promotion-time model: the plateau comes from a capped cumulative hazard. With F(t) a distribution function and θ > 0, S(t) = exp[−θ × F(t)] and the cure fraction is e−θ.
  • It is available in flexsurvcure with mixture = FALSE.
  • They are related but not interchangeable. With the same distribution family they fit differently, and a covariate effect means something different in each. Your HTA tutorial calls them "mathematically equivalent". I would soften that, and section 13 explains why.

2 · How we get π

Where the cure fraction comes from

The cure fraction is not measured. Nobody is tested for "cure". It is inferred from the shape of the curve. There are three routes, from intuition to what software really does.

Route A: read it from the plateau (the intuition)

At the end of follow-up, time T, the model says S(T) = π + (1 − π) × Su(T). Solve for π:

π = ( S(T) − S_u(T) ) / ( 1 − S_u(T) )

In words: take the height of the curve at the end, remove the uncured people who are still waiting for their event, and rescale.

  • If follow-up is long, Su(T) is close to 0 and π is almost exactly the plateau height.
  • If follow-up is short, Su(T) is large, and the plateau height overstates π.

Worked example (the fitted models from my simulation in section 8, true π = 0.40):

Follow-up TKM at TFitted Su(T)π = (S − Su) / (1 − Su)Fitted π̂
8 years0.45670.1618(0.4567 − 0.1618) / 0.8382 = 0.3520.352

Both lines return the fitted value exactly. In this simulation all censoring happens on the stop date, so the fitted curve passes through the KM end value and the formula is exact. In real data with scattered censoring it is an approximation, so treat it as a way to understand the estimate, not to compute it.

Route B: maximum likelihood (what software does)

Maximum likelihood means: try every candidate value of the parameters and keep the ones that make the observed data most probable. Each patient contributes one term.

  • A patient who had the event at time ti must be uncured. Their contribution is the density (1 − π) × f_u(t_i).
  • A patient who was censored at time ti is still event-free, and could be cured or uncured. Their contribution is S(t_i) = π + (1 − π) × S_u(t_i).

With di = 1 for an event and di = 0 for censoring, the likelihood L and its logarithm ℓ are:

L = Π_i [ (1 − π) f_u(t_i) ]^d_i × [ π + (1 − π) S_u(t_i) ]^(1 − d_i) ℓ = Σ_i { d_i × ln[ (1 − π) f_u(t_i) ] + (1 − d_i) × ln[ π + (1 − π) S_u(t_i) ] }

The software searches for the π, λ and γ that make ℓ as large as possible. π is kept between 0 and 1 by estimating it on the logit scale:

π = 1 / (1 + e^(−β0)), β0 = ln( π / (1 − π) )

Toy example by hand. Three patients: one event at year 1, one censored at year 5, one censored at year 10. Use λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5 and try five values of π.

How to read it:

  • Row π = 0.45, event term: fu(1) = 0.30 × 1.5 × 10.5 / (1 + 0.30)2 = 0.45 / 1.69 = 0.2663. Times (1 − 0.45) = 0.55 gives 0.1464.
  • Row π = 0.45, censored at 5: S(5) = 0.45 + 0.55 × 0.2297 = 0.5763.
  • The log-likelihood adds the logs: ln 0.1464 + ln 0.5763 + ln 0.5024 = −1.921 − 0.551 − 0.688 = −3.160.

Same three patients, λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5. Slide π and watch the log-likelihood climb to a peak and fall again.

Standard errors and intervals

  • The curvature of ℓ around its peak tells you the precision: a sharp peak means a precise estimate, a flat peak means a vague one.
  • A Wald interval is built from that curvature on the logit scale: logit(π̂) ± 1.96 × SE.
  • A profile likelihood interval is better when the peak is flat. Keep every π whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the maximum (half of 3.84, the 95% cut-off of a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom). It can be lopsided, which is honest.

Route C: bring π from outside (the informed approach)

When trial follow-up is too short, take π from other evidence and either fix it or give it a narrow range.

  • Registry or long-term follow-up: apply the plateau formula of Route A to long-term relative survival (patient survival divided by general-population survival).
  • Expert opinion: a documented clinician panel (as in NICE TA975, where the company compared fits with clinician cure estimates).
  • Bayesian version: put a prior distribution on π centred on the outside evidence.
  • Always run it for a range of π and show the results as scenarios.

Cure fraction with covariates

To let the cure fraction depend on treatment (z = 1 for treated, 0 for control), use a logistic regression for π:

π(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−(β0 + β1 × z)))

Example: control π = 0.30 gives β0 = ln(0.30 / 0.70) = −0.847. Treated π = 0.60 gives β0 + β1 = ln(0.60 / 0.40) = 0.405, so β1 = 1.253. The odds of cure are multiplied by e1.253 = 3.5 in the treated arm.

Who estimates π: the model or you?

In the usual setup the model estimates π from your data. You never type it in. The software treats π as one more unknown, next to the scale λ and shape γ of the latency curve, tries many combinations (Route B), and keeps the one that makes your data most probable. The clues it uses are the height of the flat tail, how long it stays flat, and how many patients are still at risk there.

The model can only do this well if you supply the right ingredients:

You supplyWhy the model cannot guess it
The latency family (Weibull, log-logistic, log-normal)Different families give different π on the same data (13% to 18% in Felizzi et al.).
Background mortality (life table), if π should mean "cured of the disease"Without it, other-cause deaths look like disease events (next subsection).
Enough follow-upWith a short window "cured" and "event not yet happened" look the same. The software still prints a number, but the interval is huge (the 3-year case in section 8).
Outside evidence, when the trial is shortRegistry or clinician estimates can fix or bound π (Route C).

A decision process, in order:

  1. Is follow-up long compared with how fast the uncured have their events, and is the flat tail well populated? If yes, let the model estimate π (Route B).
  2. Do you have outside evidence? If yes, check the estimate against it. If the trial is too short for step 1, fix or bound π from outside instead (Route C) and show scenarios.
  3. Have you dealt with background mortality? If not, use one of the two fixes in the next subsection.
  4. Report π̂ with its interval and say which approach you used. HTA reviewers will ask.

Does π take the survival of the general population into account?

π itself does not. It is a plain share of patients: the fraction who will never have this disease's event. The general population enters through a separate term, Sb(t), the survival of people of the same age and sex taken from a life table. The two multiply:

S_o(t) = S_b(t) × [ π + (1 − π) × S_u(t) ]
  • Cured patients are free of the disease but still die of other causes, so they follow Sb(t) only.
  • Uncured patients follow Sb(t) times their disease curve Su(t).
  • In hazard terms, and when the two kinds of death are independent, the overall hazard is the sum of the two:
h_o(t) = h_b(t) + h(t), where h(t) = (1 − π) × f_u(t) / S(t)

Worked example. Take π = 0.45, λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5, and a background death rate of 1% per year, so Sb(t) = e−0.01t (an older population, for illustration).

How to read it:

  • The disease-only curve levels off at π. The observed curve keeps sliding, because the cured patients keep dying of other causes.
  • With the starting values, reading the observed curve at year 30 as if it were the plateau gives 0.34, not 0.45. The model cannot tell "a cured patient died of old age" from "an uncured patient had the disease event", because both are deaths.
  • Dividing the observed curve by Sb(t) brings back the disease-only curve (last column). It tends to π as the last uncured patients leave. At year 40 it is 0.457, because a few uncured patients are still alive.

Two fixes, so that π means "cured of the disease":

  1. Relative survival. Divide patient survival by the survival expected in the general population, then fit the cure model to that. The cuRe package works this way.
  2. Put Sb(t) inside the likelihood. The model then knows that some deaths are ordinary deaths. Each patient needs a matching life-table rate (age, sex, country, year). The event term of the likelihood uses the overall hazard hb(ti) + h(ti) and the survival term uses So(ti).

What to expect in practice:

  • Young patients and short follow-up: the problem is small.
  • Older patients or 20 or more years of follow-up: ignoring background deaths tends to push π̂ down and to distort the latency curve. The size depends on the latency family, so I did not put a single number on it.
  • In the economic model, put Sb(t) back in, so cured patients still die at normal rates. Without it, cured patients never die and life-years are overstated. Test an excess-risk multiplier on Sb(t) as a scenario (see pitfall 5).
3 · Who counts as cured

How the model defines a patient as cured

Short answer: it does not decide for any individual. "Cured" is a hidden label. The model estimates how likely each survivor is to carry it.

The definition

Give every patient a hidden (latent) label Zi:

Z_i = 1 (cured) with probability π Z_i = 0 (uncured) with probability 1 − π
  • If Zi = 1, the event time is infinite: the patient never has the event from this disease.
  • If Zi = 0, the event time follows the latency curve Su(t), which eventually reaches zero. This is the condition that makes the uncured group "proper".
  • With background mortality, cured patients still die at the general-population rate, so "cured" means no excess risk from the disease.

What the data tell us about the label

We never observe Zi. We observe only whether an event happened.

  • A patient with an event is certainly uncured. Cured patients have no events, so P(cured | event) = 0.
  • A patient who is censored is a mixture of possibilities. They are either cured, or uncured and not yet due for an event. By Bayes' rule (a way to update a probability after seeing evidence), a patient censored at time c has:
P(cured | censored at c) = π / S(c) P(uncured | censored at c) = (1 − π) × S_u(c) / S(c)

How software "decides": the EM algorithm

Semiparametric packages such as smcure use the EM algorithm (expectation-maximisation: alternate between guessing the hidden labels and refitting the model).

  • E-step (guess). Give each patient a weight wi equal to the probability that they are uncured.
w_i = 1 if patient i had an event w_i = (1 − π) S_u(t_i) / [ π + (1 − π) S_u(t_i) ] if patient i was censored
  • M-step (refit). Update the cure fraction as the average probability of being cured, and refit the latency curve with each patient weighted by wi.
π_new = (1 / n) × Σ_i (1 − w_i)
  • Repeat until the numbers stop changing.
  • Numeric peek: a patient censored at year 5 with the current π = 0.45 gets w = 0.219. They count as 0.781 of a cured patient and 0.219 of an uncured patient in the next update.

Can I label individuals?

  • You can say "patients with probability above 0.9 are probably cured", but that is a modelling statement, not a diagnosis.
  • HTA submissions do not need individual labels. They need π and the curve.
  • The probability depends on the model's latency assumption. Change the latency family and the same survivor gets a different probability.

What the model has to believe

  1. The two groups exist from the start and do not change.
  2. Cured patients never have the event from this disease.
  3. Every uncured patient would eventually have the event, if followed long enough.
  4. Long-term event-free survivors are cured only if the latency curve says an uncured patient would normally have had the event by then. The assumed tail shape is what separates "cured" from "slow". This is why π changes with the latency family (13% to 18% in Felizzi et al.).
Statistical cure is not biological cure

In a mixture model, "cured" means a zero disease-related event hazard in the fitted model. Late relapses beyond the follow-up window would be invisible. In registry work, "statistical cure" means the excess mortality over the general population has returned to zero. Say which one you mean.

4 · The cure point

How the model finds the cure point

Short answer: it does not. A mixture cure model has no cure-point parameter. Cured patients are cured from time zero. What people call a "cure point" is a time you derive after fitting, using a rule you choose.

Why there is no switch

  • The curve S(t) = π + (1 − π) × Su(t) is smooth at every t.
  • The hazard h(t) declines smoothly toward zero (or toward the background rate).
  • There is no moment at which patients change status. What changes is our certainty about who is left.

Three ways to define a cure point after the fit

Rule 1: a probability threshold. Pick a certainty level q (for example 95%) and find the time when P(cured | event-free at t) = q. Since this probability equals π / S(t), the target is S(t*) = π / q. Solving for the uncured survival at that time:

S_u* = π × (1/q − 1) / (1 − π)

For the log-logistic latency this inverts to:

t* = ( (1 / S_u* − 1) / λ ) ^ (1 / γ)

Worked example (π = 0.45, λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5, q = 0.95):

  • Su* = 0.45 × (1/0.95 − 1) / 0.55 = 0.45 × 0.05263 / 0.55 = 0.04306
  • 1 / Su* − 1 = 23.22 − 1 = 22.22, divided by λ: 22.22 / 0.30 = 74.07
  • t* = 74.071/1.5 = 74.070.667, which is 17.6 years

Rule 2: a hazard threshold. Choose the time when the hazard h(t) falls below a limit, for example the background hazard of the population, or an absolute value you can defend.

Rule 3: a clinical rule. Clinicians state when a patient is usually considered cured. In NICE TA975 the committee said a person is cured of B-ALL if they have had no B-ALL-related event for around 7 years. That is an input from outside the model, used to judge or support it.

What this means in an economic model

  • With an MCM plus background mortality you do not need a cure point. Cured patients follow the general-population hazard from day one, automatically. Uncured patients carry their latency hazard, which fades.
  • A cure point is needed when you do not use an MCM, for example in a single cure point model that follows the trial curve until year X and then switches everyone to background mortality. That model forces the choice, and the choice moves the result (section 8, example 2).
  • Where a cure time is still useful:
    • To communicate: "among patients event-free at 5 years, about 78% are expected to be cured".
    • To validate: compare the model's conditional survival with registry data from the same time.
    • To stage an excess-risk assumption, such as extra background hazard for survivors.
  • Registry papers also define a "time to cure" with threshold rules of this type. The exact thresholds are analyst choices, and I did not verify specific published values.
5 · When to use it

A decision process, not a checklist of reasons

Ask these questions in this order. Each one can stop you. Earlier questions are about biology and are cheap; later ones are about data and are expensive.

  1. Is cure biologically believable for this disease and this treatment?
    Ask a clinician. Good signs: durable complete remission after CAR-T, long-term responders on immunotherapy, cure after curative surgery. If clinicians say "everyone eventually progresses", stop. An MCM would invent a cure group that does not exist.
  2. Does the KM curve really flatten?
    Look at the tail. You want a long flat stretch with few events and enough patients still at risk in that stretch. Always plot the numbers at risk under the curve. A flat tail built on 6 patients is not a plateau.
  3. Is follow-up long enough, compared with how fast the uncured have their events?
    If the uncured typically have events within 2 years, a plateau that has lasted 5 years means something. If the uncured can relapse after 6 years, a plateau at year 5 proves nothing. The simulation in section 8 shows this.
  4. Can you check π against outside evidence?
    Registry survival, longer follow-up publications, or a documented clinician panel. If yes, fit the model freely and compare ("uninformed" approach). If the trial is too short, you can fix π from outside evidence ("informed" approach) and present it as a scenario.
  5. Would the answer change the decision?
    Run both a cure and a non-cure model and look at the ICER (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio: extra cost per extra quality-adjusted life-year, or QALY). If it barely moves, keep the simpler model.

6 · Data needed

Surprisingly little data, but it must be mature

Felizzi et al. note that "relatively few data are required". The catch is that the data must be long enough. Think of it in three layers.

What "mature" looks like

  • A flat tail that covers a meaningful stretch of time, not just the last few months.
  • Patients still at risk inside that flat part.
  • Follow-up that is long compared with the uncured group's typical time to event. In my simulation the median time to event for the uncured was 2.2 years, and π was only reasonably pinned down after about 8 years. That is a rule of thumb from one simulated dataset, not a law.
If you only have a published curve

You can rebuild approximate patient-level data from a digitised KM curve, but the tail is the weakest part of that process. Without the numbers at risk, you cannot tell a real plateau from a few lucky survivors. Treat any π from reconstructed data as a scenario that needs outside support.

7 · How to use it

The workflow, then the code

  1. Plot the KM curve with numbers at risk. Confirm there is a plateau and that people are still at risk in it.
  2. Fit standard models with no cure first. These are your comparators. If they already reach near zero at a believable time, you may not need an MCM.
  3. Pick several latency distributions. Weibull, log-logistic and log-normal are the usual trio. The choice changes π.
  4. Fit the MCM for each one by maximum likelihood (choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable).
  5. Compare. Use AIC (a score that rewards fit and penalises extra parameters; lower is better), visual fit to the KM curve, the estimated π with its confidence interval, and the shape of the hazard.
  6. Validate π and the tail against registry or long-term data.
  7. Add background mortality and decide how cured patients are handled in the economic model.
  8. Report the choice, the alternatives, and a scenario analysis.

Code A: the package route in R

High level: fit a no-cure log-logistic and a log-logistic mixture cure model to the same data, compare them, then loop over three latency families.

library(survival)       # Surv() and survfit()

Line by line:

  • library(survival): loads the core survival tools. Surv() bundles time and event into one survival object.
  • library(flexsurv): gives flexsurvreg() for standard parametric models.
  • library(flexsurvcure): adds flexsurvcure(), built on flexsurv, for cure models.
  • survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ 1, data = df): the KM estimate for everyone together (the ~ 1 means "no grouping"). plot(km) draws it.
  • flexsurvreg(..., dist = "llogis"): a log-logistic with no cure group. This is your comparator.
  • flexsurvcure(..., mixture = TRUE, link = "logistic"): the same latency family plus a cure fraction. The logistic link keeps π between 0 and 1 by estimating it on the logit scale (logit(π) = log(π / (1 − π))).
  • AIC(fit_ll, fit_mcm): lower is better. A gap of more than about 2 means a real difference.
  • summary(fit_mcm, t = c(5, 10, 20), type = "survival"): survival at the chosen years, including beyond the data. That is the extrapolation.
  • The lapply loop: refits the MCM for each family. sapply(fits, AIC) collects the AICs. f$res["theta", "est"] pulls the cure fraction estimate.

Code B: build the likelihood yourself (Python, run and tested)

High level: the MCM is just a likelihood with two kinds of patient contributions. Patients who had the event contribute the density f(t). Censored patients contribute the survival S(t). Maximise the sum of the logs. This is exactly what I ran for the simulation in section 8, and it is the code form of the log-likelihood ℓ in section 2.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def neg_loglik(p, time, event):
    pi  = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-p[0]))                         # cure fraction, forced into (0, 1)
    lam = np.exp(p[1])                                    # latency scale, forced positive
    gam = np.exp(p[2])                                    # latency shape, forced positive
    Su  = 1 / (1 + lam * time**gam)                       # survival of the uncured
    fu  = lam * gam * time**(gam - 1) / (1 + lam * time**gam)**2   # density of the uncured
    S   = pi + (1 - pi) * Su                              # overall survival
    f   = (1 - pi) * fu                                   # overall density
    ll  = event * np.log(f) + (1 - event) * np.log(S)     # events use f, censored use S
    return -ll.sum()                                      # minimise the negative

fit    = minimize(neg_loglik, x0=[0.0, -1.0, 0.4], args=(time, event), method="Nelder-Mead")
pi_hat = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-fit.x[0]))

Line by line:

  • neg_loglik(p, time, event): takes three unconstrained numbers p and the data. The optimiser needs unconstrained inputs, so each parameter is transformed.
  • pi = 1/(1+exp(-p[0])): the inverse logit. Any real number becomes a valid probability.
  • lam = exp(p[1]), gam = exp(p[2]): exponentials keep both parameters positive.
  • Su and fu: the log-logistic survival and density for the uncured. The density is the derivative of 1 − Su.
  • S and f: the mixture survival and the mixture density. The density gets the factor (1 − π) because cured patients never have an event.
  • ll: event is 1 or 0, so each patient adds exactly one of the two log terms.
  • minimize(...): finds the p that makes the negative log-likelihood smallest. x0 is the starting guess. In the full simulation I used several starting guesses and kept the best, because mixture likelihoods can be flat and have local bumps.
  • pi_hat: converts the fitted first parameter back to a probability.

The informed approach in one change: to fix the cure fraction from outside evidence, hold p[0] at logit(πexternal) and optimise only the other two parameters. Run it for a range of π values and show the results as scenarios.

library(smcure)

8 · Worked calculation

Three examples, from pencil to published

Example 1: the hand calculation

Setup. 1,000 patients. Suppose π = 0.45, so 450 are cured and 550 are uncured. The uncured follow a log-logistic latency:

S_u(t) = 1 / (1 + λ × t^γ) with λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5 S(t) = 0.45 + 0.55 × S_u(t)
Correction to your source file

Your file prints Su(t) = 1 / (1 + (λ·t)γ), but its table only works with 1 / (1 + λ·tγ). At t = 1 the table shows 0.300, which is λ·tγ, not (λ·t)γ = 0.164. I used the version that matches the table. These numbers are illustrative: they are not the cure fraction from NICE TA975, whose text reports no tisagenlecleucel value.

Step 1: one row, slowly (year 5).

  • tγ = 51.5 = 11.18
  • λ·tγ = 0.30 × 11.18 = 3.354
  • Su(5) = 1 / (1 + 3.354) = 1 / 4.354 = 0.230 (23% of the uncured are still event-free)
  • S(5) = 0.45 + 0.55 × 0.230 = 0.576, so about 576 of 1,000 are alive and event-free
  • P(cured | alive) = 0.45 / 0.576 = 78.1%
  • Hazard: fu(5) = 0.30 × 1.5 × 50.5 / 4.3542 = 1.006 / 18.96 = 0.0531. Then h(5) = 0.55 × 0.0531 / 0.576 = 0.0506 per year.

Example 2: why a hard "cure point" costs you

A single cure point model follows the trial curve until a chosen year, then treats every survivor as cured. Suppose it is set at year 7.

  • At year 7 there are 534 survivors. Only 450 are truly cured.
  • So 84 uncured patients (534 − 450) are wrongly handed a zero excess risk.
  • The MCM keeps their small leftover hazard (0.029 per year at year 7) and lets it die away smoothly.

To see the money effect, add a small background death rate of 0.1% per year (a young population, illustrative) and a 40-year horizon:

Example 3: fitting an MCM to simulated trial data

Design. I simulated 300 patients. The true cure fraction was 0.40 (113 of the 300 were cured by chance, so 0.377 in this sample). The uncured followed the same log-logistic (λ = 0.30, γ = 1.5). I then "stopped the trial" at six different follow-up lengths and refitted the MCM each time. This mimics what happens as a real trial matures. The true survival at 20 years is 0.422.

What it teaches:

  • Up to 3 years: π̂ jumps around (0.48, 0.39, 0.03) and the intervals are huge. The MCM does not fit better than the no-cure model. The data simply cannot tell the two stories apart.
  • The 2-year S(20) of 0.423 looks perfect. It is luck: the interval for π̂ at that point runs from 0.05 to 0.89. Never trust a good-looking extrapolation without its uncertainty.
  • At 8 years the MCM clearly wins (AIC gain 12) and the interval tightens. At 15 years the estimate lands on the truth.
  • The no-cure model is too pessimistic at every stage: it predicts 15% to 31% survival at 20 years against a truth of 42%.
  • This echoes the formal simulation by Kearns et al. (2021): with the shortest follow-up, none of the cure models extrapolated well.

11 · Caveats and pitfalls

How MCMs go wrong

PitfallWhy it happensWhat to do
1. Follow-up too shortThe model has to tell "cured" from "event not yet happened". With a short window both look identical, so π is nearly unconstrained (see the profile chart in section 2 and the simulation in section 8).Show π̂ with its interval. Do not call the MCM base case unless the plateau is long. Use an informed scenario instead.
2. A flat tail caused by censoringLate in a KM curve few patients are at risk, so one censoring or one event moves the curve a lot. A "plateau" can be only noise.Always show numbers at risk. Check the confidence band, not just the line.
3. Latency distribution drives πEach family explains the slowly falling tail in its own way, and part of that tail is "cure" in one family but "slow decline" in another. So π̂ moves with the family. In Felizzi et al., π̂ ranged from 13% to 18% across families fitted to the same simulated data.Fit several families. Report the range of π̂ and the extrapolated survival from each.
4. Trusting AIC aloneAIC measures fit to the observed window. The tail you care about is outside it. Felizzi et al. warn that flat tails can make AIC less suitable.Combine AIC with hazard plots, long-term external data and clinical opinion.
5. Forgetting background mortalityWithout Sb(t) the cured group never dies, which is wrong. Cured patients also may not match the general population exactly. One NICE appraisal assumed about 40% higher background hazard for cancer survivors (Felizzi et al.).Use country, age, sex and year specific life tables. Test an excess-mortality multiplier as a scenario.
6. Reading "cure" too literallyThe model is statistical. It does not prove biological cure, and some "cured" patients may relapse very late.Say "long-term survivors" or "statistical cure" in the text and include a late-relapse scenario.
7. Two-group assumption breaksIf patients can be cured at any time, not only up front, a fixed split into two groups from time zero is a poor description.Discuss with clinicians. Consider other structures or a spline scenario.
8. Covariates and treatment effectThe treatment can change π, or the timing among the uncured, or both. Mixing them up changes the long-term gain.State which parameters differ by arm, and test both choices.
9. Parameter confusionSoftware defines log-logistic differently. In flexsurv, S(t) = 1/(1 + (t/scale)shape), so the λ in this page equals scale−shape. Copying a λ from one tool to another gives wrong curves.Write the formula you used and convert carefully.
10. Pooled data mismatchCombining trial and real-world data (as in TA975) is helpful, but the populations may differ.Compare baseline characteristics and show each source alongside the pooled fit.
11. Unvalidated πA π that conflicts with registries gets rejected (for example 60% cure where registries show about 20% five-year survival).Build the external check into the plan before you fit.
12. Choosing the cure point by habitUsing "year 7" or "year 5" because others did. The certainty at that time may be modest (84% at year 7 in the example in section 4).State the rule (probability, hazard or clinical) and show the sensitivity.
12 · Use in HTA

How an MCM is judged in a submission

Where it sits

  • Base case when cure is clinically accepted and the data support it (TA975).
  • Scenario when cure is possible but π cannot be confirmed. The standard parametric model or spline stays the base case.
  • Not at all when clinicians expect late relapses and there is no plateau. TA567 stayed with a 1-knot spline.

Recommended workflow for a NICE-style submission

  1. Check the KM curve for a plateau, few events at the end, and a confidence band that allows a flat line.
  2. Compare with standard models. If they already drop to near zero at a believable time, an MCM may be unnecessary.
  3. Fit MCMs across several latency families (Weibull, log-logistic, log-normal at least). Compare AIC and the estimated π from each.
  4. Validate π against registry survival, long-term publications and a documented expert panel.
  5. Choose the role (base case or scenario) and write down why.

What the evidence review group will probably ask

NICE's external group is now called the EAG (External Assessment Group). Older documents say ERG. Same idea.

  • What external data support the cure fraction, and how closely does the model match them?
  • Who defined the cure point or the tail, and was that decided before looking at the results?
  • How sensitive are QALYs and the ICER to π, to the latency family and to the general population mortality used?
  • Were trial and real-world data pooled, and did you compare them?
  • How is uncertainty in π carried into the probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA: running the model many times with parameters drawn from their distributions)?

Linking it to the model

  • In a partitioned survival model (a model that splits time into states such as event-free, progressed, dead using fitted survival curves), the MCM supplies the OS or EFS curve. Cured patients then follow background mortality, ideally with an excess-risk option.
  • PSA draws: take parameters on the estimation scale (logit for π, log for scales and shapes), draw from a multivariate normal using the covariance matrix of the fit, then back-transform. Check the object names in your package version.
  • Discounting: in TA975 the committee did not accept the lower 1.5% discount rate, because uncertainty about cure meant the criteria were only partly met.
  • Reporting: give the table of all fitted models, the π̂ and interval for each, the extrapolated survival and restricted mean survival time at the horizon, and the external comparison.
Other agencies

Your source file says CADTH has "generally accepted" 1-knot splines or MCMs. I could not verify a CADTH-specific position, so I left it out. NICE DSU TSD 21 on flexible survival methods is a good anchor, but please check the exact section on cure models before quoting it.

Reporting checklist

  • KM curve with numbers at risk, plateau described in words.
  • Clinical justification for cure, with the name of the expert source.
  • All latency families fitted, with AIC, π̂ and interval.
  • External validation of π and of survival at 5 and 10 years.
  • Rule used for any cure point (probability, hazard or clinical) and its sensitivity.
  • Background mortality source and any excess-risk multiplier.
  • Scenario table: π low, base, high; alternative families; no-cure comparator.
  • ICER and QALY change for each scenario.

Where we agree, and a few things to tidy

Where we agree. Your files get the core right: the form S(t) = π + (1 − π)Su(t), the conditional cure probability π / S(t), the hazard formula, the contrast with a single cure point, and the rule that π must be validated against outside data. I built this page on those.

Where I would adjust, offered as a co-author would. In order of importance:

  1. Formula vs table. The printed latency formula uses (λ·t)γ, but the table uses λ·tγ. Pick one and state it. I used the table's version.
  2. The 0.45 cure fraction is illustrative. It reads like a TA975 or ELIANA estimate. The NICE text I retrieved reports no tisagenlecleucel cure value, so I would label it "hypothetical".
  3. TA559 is mislabelled. In the spline file, TA559 sits under "Tisagenlecleucel for Pediatric B-ALL". Per the NICE listings I found, it is an axicabtagene ciloleucel appraisal (DLBCL). Please verify on the NICE site before reuse.
  4. R argument names. anc = list(cure = ~ treat) and coef(fit)["cure"] do not match what I could confirm (theta, and formula covariates). Worth a quick test.
  5. "Equivalent" mixture and non-mixture. They are related, not interchangeable. I would say "alternative ways to build a plateau".
  6. "MCM preferred over any spline when a plateau is visible" and "1 knot is supported by every relevant TA". TA567 accepted a 1-knot spline and TA554/559 used MCMs, which supports a more careful statement: plateau plus biology plus validation favours an MCM. Kearns et al. also find that with immature data you cannot reliably choose, so clinical knowledge decides.
  7. The "cure point" language. Your file describes the MCM as handling a "transition", which is right, but a reader may still ask "when does the model say patients are cured?". Section 4 answers that: it never does, and any cure time comes from a rule you choose.
  8. Reference list. Entry 16 in the spline file is a news site about an unrelated cancer. Remove it before circulating. Also, I could not find the Whittington et al. 2018 record in PubMed by citation, so I did not use it as an example.

Glossary

Terms in one line each

Open the glossary
  • AIC: a score balancing fit against number of parameters. Lower is better.
  • Background mortality: death rate of the general population of the same age and sex.
  • Bayes' rule: a way to update a probability after seeing evidence. Used to get the chance a survivor is cured.
  • Censoring: we only know a patient was event-free up to their last follow-up.
  • Cure fraction (π): share of patients who will never have the event from this disease.
  • EAG / ERG: the independent group that reviews a company submission for NICE.
  • EM algorithm: alternates between guessing hidden labels and refitting the model.
  • Hazard: instantaneous risk of the event among those still event-free.
  • ICER: extra cost per extra QALY gained.
  • Kaplan-Meier curve: step-shaped estimate of the survival curve from data.
  • Latency distribution: the curve of event times for the uncured patients.
  • Life table: published death rates by age (and sex, country, year).
  • Logit: log(p / (1 − p)), a way to keep a probability between 0 and 1.
  • Maximum likelihood: choosing the parameters that make the observed data most probable.
  • Partitioned survival model: a model that sets state occupancy using fitted survival curves.
  • Profile likelihood: the likelihood of one parameter after optimising all others. Gives honest, lopsided intervals.
  • PSA: running the model many times with parameters drawn from their uncertainty.
  • QALY: quality-adjusted life-year.
  • Relative survival: patient survival compared with the expected survival of the general population.
  • Statistical cure: excess mortality has returned to zero, whether or not the disease is biologically gone.
