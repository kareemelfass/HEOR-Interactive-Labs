1. Definition of unmet medical need

Unmet medical need (UMN) is a clinically important health need that remains inadequately addressed by existing healthcare interventions. There is no universally accepted definition [1]. The central question is not whether a treatment exists, but whether current care adequately addresses what patients need.

DimensionWhat remains unmet
AvailabilityNo effective treatment
EfficacyInsufficient benefit
ResponseNon-response
DurabilityRelapse or resistance
SafetySignificant adverse effects
TolerabilityInability to continue treatment
Population coverageNo suitable option for a subgroup
Quality of lifePersistent symptoms or functional limitations
Treatment burdenAdministration or monitoring burden
DiagnosisInaccurate or late diagnosis
PreventionInadequate prevention
AccessibilityEffective care exists but is inaccessible

Distinguish therapeutic inadequacy from an access gap: if a medicine works but is not funded, improving coverage may be more efficient than developing another medicine.

2. Distinguishing related concepts

These terms describe different questions. Disease severity and burden concern the problem; UMN concerns the inadequacy of existing care; added benefit concerns the improvement offered by the new intervention.

ConceptQuestion
SeverityHow serious is the condition?
Disease burdenHow much health is lost individually or across the population?
UMNWhat remains inadequately addressed?
Added benefitWhat does the new technology improve?
InnovationWhat is new?
Cost-effectivenessIs additional benefit worth the additional cost?
AffordabilityCan adoption be financed?
EquityShould certain patients or needs receive additional priority?

2.1 Severity versus unmet need

Two diseases have untreated remaining life expectancy of 10 years and a reference of 40 years. With treatment, A reaches 38 years and B reaches 14 years. The residual gaps are 2 and 26 years. Severity may also be defined under current care, so severity and UMN can overlap.

Survival is one dimension of UMN, not a comprehensive measure.

2.2 Added benefit versus unmet need

Median overall survival (OS)Setting ASetting B
Current care6 months24 months
New treatment7 months36 months
Difference in medians1 month12 months

Setting A may have greater survival-related need but a smaller observed improvement. Differences in medians do not establish mean survival gains or total clinical value; safety, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and uncertainty also matter.

2.3 Rarity and innovation

Neither rarity nor a first-in-class mechanism guarantees high UMN or meaningful clinical benefit. A common disease can contain a poorly served subgroup.

The magnitude of the problem is not the magnitude of the solution.

3. Stakeholder perspectives

Vreman et al. identified 16 definitions of UMN; key dimensions include the adequacy of existing treatments, disease severity or burden, and sometimes population size [1]. The purpose of the decision shapes the definition.

StakeholderQuestion

CliniciansWho cannot be adequately managed?
RegulatorsDoes the technology potentially address a serious therapeutic gap?
HTA agenciesWhat additional value does it provide versus alternatives?
PayersCan this use of resources be justified?
PolicymakersWhich needs should receive priority and investment?
IndustryWhich gaps justify research investment?

Regulatory approval versus HTA

FDA expedited programmes and EMA conditional marketing authorisation can facilitate development or access under defined conditions [2,3]. Regulatory assessment concerns benefit-risk; HTA concerns comparative value and consequences of adoption. Expedited approval does not establish cost-effectiveness, affordability or a favourable reimbursement decision.

4. Position within the HTA framework

UMN is not just a step in a linear process. It establishes the context for assessment and may inform appraisal, where evidence is interpreted alongside the values and priorities of the health system.

Disease and patient populationSeverity, epidemiology, symptoms and burden
↓
Current standard of careAlternatives and limitations
↓
Residual unmet medical needWhat remains unresolved?
↓
Clinical assessmentEffectiveness, safety, certainty
Economic assessmentCost-effectiveness and budget impact
↓
HTA appraisalEvidence, unmet need, severity, equity and uncertainty
↓
Recommendation and policyPricing, reimbursement, restrictions and access

Conceptual synthesis, not a universal agency workflow. Assessment evaluates evidence; appraisal interprets it to formulate recommendations.

5. Uses within HTA

5.1 Topic prioritisation

Assessment resources are limited. Earlier assessment of potentially beneficial technologies may reduce delays. The EU HTA Regulation includes UMN in selection criteria for certain devices and prioritisation of joint scientific consultations [4]. Priority does not guarantee reimbursement.

5.2 Defining the decision problem

Consider rheumatoid arthritis: the general population may have many biologic options, whereas patients who have failed several therapies may have few suitable options. The comparator, expected outcomes and residual need differ. Define the actual indication, treatment line and subgroup.

5.3 Selecting outcomes

NeedRelevant evidence
Poor survivalOS, survival probabilities
ProgressionProgression-free survival (PFS), progression events
SymptomsSymptom scores, patient-reported outcomes
RelapseRelapse rate, duration of response
ToxicityAdverse events, discontinuation
FunctionFunctional outcomes, HRQoL
Administration burdenPatient preferences, resource use, burden

Similar efficacy with materially better safety may address important UMN.

5.4 Evidence and uncertainty

Evidence certainty is confidence in the estimated effect; uncertainty tolerance is the amount of uncertainty acceptable for a decision. A serious need may affect the latter, not the former. A single-arm study does not become equivalent to a randomised controlled trial (RCT). NICE considers uncertainty in context [5].

5.5 Economic assessment

Poor outcomes can create scope for incremental QALYs (quality-adjusted life years), fewer complications can reduce costs, and an authorised social value mechanism may give greater priority to health gains. The first two are conventional modelling; the third is a distinct social judgement.

5.6 Budget impact and uptake

UMN may affect uptake, untreated population size, expenditure, capacity and waiting lists. Ireland's NCPE guideline v1.1 (April 2026, p.14) specifies 100% market share when there is evidence of high UMN AND a reasonable expectation of general preference over current alternatives [8]. This is not a universal prediction of uptake. Eligibility, financing and capacity remain important.

7. Cost-effectiveness and the NICE severity modifier

7.1 Conventional analysis

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) expresses the extra cost per extra unit of health benefit.

\[ICER=\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Q}\]

Current care yields 2 QALYs and the new treatment 4; an incremental cost of £60,000 gives an ICER of £30,000/QALY. The clinical gain is already counted; a priority weight is a distinct valuation.

7.2 Severity adjustment

NICE measures severity using future QALY shortfall under established NHS care relative to expected remaining QALYs in a matched general population [5]. Absolute QALY shortfall (AQS) is total future health lost; proportional QALY shortfall (PQS) is the fraction lost.

\[AQS=Q_{\text{reference}}-Q_{\text{current care}}\]\[PQS=\frac{Q_{\text{reference}}-Q_{\text{current care}}}{Q_{\text{reference}}}\]
WeightAQSPQS
1.0Below 12Below 0.85
1.212 to below 180.85 to below 0.95
1.718 or above0.95 or above

Use the higher applicable category. This is the severity framework reproduced from the source tutorial, not a general UMN premium.

7.3 Worked example

\[\Delta Q_{\text{weighted}}=w\Delta Q,\qquad ICER_{\text{weighted}}=\frac{\Delta C}{w\Delta Q}\]

Experiment 5: Severity-adjusted ICER

Try reference 10 and current care 0.5: PQS reaches 95% even though AQS is 9.5. Try reference 20 and current care 6 or 10 to see the other categories.

At default inputs, AQS = 18, PQS = 90%, weight = 1.7, weighted QALYs = 3.4 and weighted ICER ≈ £17,647. Actual gain stays 2 QALYs; the additional 1.4 is not additional health.

Severity weights capture only one related dimension, not all unmet needs. A technology can address UMN without qualifying.

8. Double counting

Survival gains and reduced toxicity can already be reflected in QALYs; avoided hospitalisations can already be reflected in costs. Adding another numerical benefit for the same clinical improvement can count it twice.

ApproachMeaning
Survival improvement in QALYsMeasuring health
Reduced toxicity in QALYsMeasuring health
Avoided hospital admissions in costsMeasuring resource consequences
Authorised severity modifierAssigning social priority
Unsupported UMN premiumPossible double counting or unjustified valuation

Explicit severity weighting is not inherently double counting because its purpose differs. Stacking rarity, severity, end-of-life and UMN premiums without justification may give repeated priority to overlapping attributes.

9. Health-policy priorities

Efficiency seeks to maximise health from limited resources. Equity concerns the fairness of the distribution of those gains. Opportunity cost is the health forgone when resources are used for one intervention instead of another.

Suppose £8 million can be invested in either A (£20,000/QALY, multiple effective therapies) or B (£80,000/QALY, severe disease with no effective treatment).

\[Q_A=\frac{8{,}000{,}000}{20{,}000}=400,\quad Q_B=\frac{8{,}000{,}000}{80{,}000}=100\]\[Q_{\text{forgone}}=400-100=300\]

Experiment 6: Compare uses of a fixed budget

Should patients with greater UMN receive additional priority even when treatment is less cost-effective? This example assumes mutually exclusive programmes that can each absorb the full budget at constant ICER. It is not an empirical estimate of displaced health.

10. Managing the policy trade-off

10.1 Formal value modifiers

Explicit weights make priorities transparent but require justification. Applying a hypothetical weight to programme B does not create more clinical health.

\[Q_{A,\text{weighted}}=Q_A,\qquad Q_{B,\text{weighted}}=w_BQ_B\]\[w_B^*=\frac{Q_A}{Q_B}\]

Experiment 7: Equity weighting

Uses the programme inputs in chapter 9. At default values, the break-even weight is 4.

Theoretical equity weights, not official NICE or other HTA weights.

10.2 Price negotiation

If incremental cost is £160,000 for 2 QALYs, the ICER is £80,000. At an illustrative acceptable level of £30,000/QALY, maximum incremental cost is £60,000, requiring a £100,000 reduction.

\[\Delta C_{\max}=\lambda\Delta Q,\qquad R=\max(0,\Delta C-\Delta C_{\max})\]

Experiment 8: Negotiation target

The illustrative level is not a statement of the current NICE threshold. A reduction in incremental total cost is not necessarily the same as a reduction in acquisition price.

10.3 Managed access

NICE Cancer Drugs Fund and Innovative Medicines Fund can support access for eligible medicines while resolving relevant uncertainties [6]. High UMN alone does not establish eligibility.

10.4 Restricted reimbursement

Funding may focus on patients who have failed existing treatments or are expected to obtain greater benefit. Improved cost-effectiveness and affordability require evidence, not assumption.

10.5 Improving access

Expand coverage, diagnostic capacity, procurement, reduce waiting times and improve adherence when the gap is delivery rather than therapeutic efficacy.

Case: Yescarta, Netherlands (2019)

The assessment of axicabtagene ciloleucel for specified relapsed/refractory lymphomas identified considerable disease burden and limited effective further treatment. Uncertain long-term survival prevented reliable cost-effectiveness estimation; the institute considered the actual ICER likely to exceed the relevant reference value [9]. High need did not remove the economic question.

12. Innovation policy

UMN has a static role (which existing interventions to fund today) and a dynamic role (which interventions to incentivise for tomorrow). Regulatory incentives, targeted scientific advice, research funding and reimbursement mechanisms can promote development in underserved areas.

Reward meaningful therapeutic advances, not novelty or an underserved indication alone. Otherwise high prices and limited incremental benefits may be incentivised.

13. Building an HTA submission argument

Make the UMN claim specific to the patient population, evidence-based and connected to comparative benefit.

StepQuestionEvidence
Define populationWho?Indication, line, characteristics
Disease burdenHow serious?Mortality, morbidity, HRQoL
Current careWhich options?Guidelines, practice, funding
Existing outcomesHow effective?Trials, registries, observational data
Residual needWhat remains?Non-response, progression, toxicity
QuantifyHow large?Absolute outcomes, affected population
Added benefitHow much is addressed?Comparative efficacy, safety, HRQoL
UncertaintyHow reliable?Evidence quality, applicability, extrapolation
EconomicsWhat cost?CEA, BIA, sensitivity analysis

Weak versus strong argument

Weak: Disease X is life-threatening; therefore additional treatment options are needed.

Stronger: Despite first- and second-line therapy, patients experience inadequate response, progression or significant toxicity. After second-line failure, options are limited and survival and HRQoL remain poor. Establish the gap in that specific population, then quantify each claim and compare the new therapy.

15. Consolidated conceptual framework

Clinical

Identify the need: burden, current care, residual health loss and patient experience.

Evidence

Assess the solution: comparative benefit, safety, applicability and certainty.

Economic

Evaluate the solution: incremental QALYs, costs, ICER, budget impact and opportunity cost.

Policy

Prioritise the solution: equity, weighting, price, reimbursement, access and innovation.

These four domains complement one another; the framework is a conceptual synthesis.

17. Final conceptual position

UMN identifies the clinical context, informs the assessment of a technology, and can influence policy priorities. It establishes the importance of the problem. Comparative evidence establishes how much a technology solves; economics evaluates the cost of achieving this gain; policy determines whether additional societal priority is warranted.
