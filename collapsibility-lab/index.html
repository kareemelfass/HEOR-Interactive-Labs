1. What is collapsibility?

A treatment effect can be calculated within a subgroup or across a whole population. A conditional contrast holds patient characteristics constant. A marginal contrast averages outcomes under each treatment over a specified target population, then calculates the contrast. These need not agree, even when treatment is randomised.

μa = Σx wx ma(x),   Σ wx = 1

Here m is an expected outcome at a given patient profile and w is the target population proportion. If the same subgroup effect θ is preserved after averaging both arms with the same weights, the measure is collapsible in this common-effect setting. More generally, collapsibility concerns whether marginal effects are meaningful weighted averages of conditional effects. [1,2]

1. Predict control outcomes for each subgroup
2. Predict treatment outcomes for the same subgroups
3. Average both sets of outcomes using the target weights
4. Calculate the marginal contrast

Do not confuse this with averaging subgroup effect estimates, or with predicting for a patient with average characteristics.

2. Why differences and risk ratios collapse

Subtraction and common multiplication preserve averaging. This is the underlying mechanism, not a special feature of a regression package.

Mean difference

Younger patients have mean blood pressure 130 under control and 120 under treatment. Older patients have 150 and 140. In an equally mixed population the means are 140 and 130, so the difference remains −10 mmHg.

μ₁ − μ₀ = Σ wx[m₁(x) − m₀(x)]

Risk difference and risk ratio

Risk is the probability of an event over a defined horizon. A risk difference (RD) is an absolute change in probability. A risk ratio (RR) compares two probabilities. If p₁(x) = r p₀(x), the common multiplier r can be moved outside the sum:

RRM = Σ wx p₁(x) / Σ wx p₀(x) = r

If subgroup RRs differ, the marginal RR uses weights proportional to wxp₀(x), not just the population proportions. RD uses the population proportions directly. A collapsible measure can still vary across populations when subgroup effects differ. Common target weights and nonzero denominators are essential.

3. Why odds ratios do not collapse

Odds are p/(1−p). A common multiplier of odds does not correspond to a common multiplier of risks. Because the transformation back to probability is nonlinear, averaging the probabilities before calculating an OR need not preserve the common subgroup OR. [1–3]

p₁(x) = r p₀(x) / [1 − p₀(x) + r p₀(x)]

With baseline risks 20% and 60%, a common OR of 0.5 produces treated risks 11.11% and 42.86%. The marginal OR is about 0.554, not 0.5. No confounding is present. Use the calculator to compare RD, RR and OR with the same baseline risks. Equal baseline risks or a null effect remove this discrepancy in this example. An OR is not a risk ratio; rare outcomes can make the difference small but not exactly zero.

4. Why hazard ratios do not collapse

A hazard is an instantaneous event rate among those who remain event-free. The denominator is not the original population. Treatment changes who survives, so even identical baseline populations develop different risk-set compositions. [3,4]

Start with 200 people per arm, half younger (low risk), half older (high risk). Control hazards are 0.10 and 0.50 per year. The conditional HR is 0.50 in both subgroups. Under exponential survival, S(t)=exp(−ht). At five years more high-risk patients survive in the treatment arm than in control.

haM(t) = Σ wxSa(t|x)ha(t|x) / Σ wxSa(t|x)

The numerator and denominator together show the weights are survival-dependent. The marginal hazard is weighted by the proportions among survivors in that arm. Explore the expected counts and hazards below; fractional counts are expectations, not observed patients.

5. Conditional PH versus marginal PH

Proportional hazards (PH) means a constant hazard ratio over time. Conditional PH does not imply marginal PH: the weights above evolve differently between arms. An unadjusted Cox coefficient under non-PH is a follow-up- and censoring-dependent partial-likelihood summary, not a simple average of instantaneous HRs. [3,4]

HRM(t) = h₁M(t) / h₀M(t)

At baseline the marginal HR equals 0.50 in the default example. At five years it is about 0.703. Treatment can improve survival at every time even if the marginal HR crosses 1 at some times in heterogeneous settings. A ratio above 1 is not proof of individual-level harm. 6. Adjustment is not conditioning

Adjustment is a procedure for accounting for baseline covariates. Conditioning describes the target contrast at a specified covariate profile. Standardisation can therefore be both adjusted and marginal. Confounding is a causal problem caused by shared determinants of exposure and outcome; non-collapsibility is a mathematical property. [1–5]

AnalysisProcedureTarget
Treatment-only CoxNo covariate adjustmentMarginal HR summary
Multivariable CoxRegression adjustmentConditional HR
IPTW, treatment-only CoxWeightingWeighted marginal HR summary
Outcome model plus standardisationRegression adjustment and averagingMarginal survival or risk

In a randomised trial, age may predict mortality without predicting allocation. An adjusted HR of 0.60 and unadjusted HR of 0.75 need not indicate confounding. Conversely, non-collapsibility does not prove an observational analysis is unbiased. Effect modification means the conditional treatment contrast varies across covariate profiles on a specified scale. It is not necessary for non-collapsibility. Do not select confounders solely from changes in OR or HR coefficients. Prespecify covariates and distinguish precision adjustment in RCTs from confounding control in observational studies.

7. From regression to population outcomes

A regression coefficient usually describes a conditional contrast. The same fitted model can be used to estimate a marginal outcome by predicting every target patient's outcome under each treatment and averaging. [3,5,6]

ŜaM(t) = (1/N) Σ Ŝ(t | A=a, Xi)

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) creates a weighted target population. For an average treatment effect, weights are A/e(X)+(1−A)/(1−e(X)), where e is the probability of receiving treatment given covariates. Balance, overlap, positivity, correct models and no unmeasured confounding remain essential. Weighted Cox under non-PH still gives a summary HR. Interactions mean the treatment coefficient alone refers to the reference covariate profile. A person-time Poisson rate ratio is not the binary RR.

8. Can we pool different reported HRs?

Meta-analysis and network meta-analysis (NMA) assume sufficiently compatible contrasts. A treatment-only Cox HR of 0.75 and an age-adjusted Cox HR of 0.65 can differ despite identical conditional efficacy. Pooling them may create a summary that corresponds to no clear target population or estimand. [3,6,7]

In an anchored indirect comparison, log HR(B versus C) = log HR(B versus A) − log HR(C versus A) is defensible only with compatible relative effects and the transitivity assumptions (comparability of the studies for the contrast). Conditional HRs must use compatible covariates and effect models; marginal HRs may vary by population and follow-up even without conditional effect modification. A random-effects model accounts for heterogeneity but does not reconcile estimands.

Extract endpoint, comparator direction, treatment strategies, adjustment variables, stratification, population characteristics, censoring, follow-up, PH assessment and uncertainty. Seek compatible estimates or synthesise survival outcomes using a coherent model. A stratified Cox estimate is conditional on strata under its model; it is not automatically marginal.

9. Pure prognostic factors can affect marginal HRs

A pure prognostic factor predicts outcome but does not change the conditional treatment effect on the chosen scale. Yet altering its distribution changes the marginal HR via the risk-set mechanism. [6–9]

MethodMechanism and caveat
MAICMatching-adjusted indirect comparison reweights IPD to match target aggregate characteristics. Inspect overlap, effective sample size and distributional balance, not only means.
STCSimulated treatment comparison uses regression; plug-in mean covariates typically produce conditional rather than marginal predictions. Integrate over a credible joint distribution for marginal predictions.
ML-NMRMultilevel network meta-regression integrates conditional models over study covariate distributions and can standardise target outcomes. Needs compatible outcome models, network identification and baseline outcome information.

For anchored comparisons, the role of prognostic factors depends on the effect scale and estimand. Do not mechanically match every prognostic variable if overlap is poor. Unanchored comparisons require stronger assumptions about absolute prognosis. Different means, distributions, correlations, and target populations can matter.

The average patient trap

Code younger patients as 0 and older as 1. A log-linear hazard model gives a hazard at the mean covariate of exp(p log λlow + (1−p) log λhigh). Predicting at that mean is not the same as averaging survival probabilities. Neither direction of error is universal.

10. Applying an HR to a KM curve

The Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve estimates marginal survival under suitable censoring assumptions. With compatible proportional hazards, cumulative hazard multiplies by r and survival becomes S₁=S₀ʳ. A conditional HR does not generally satisfy marginal PH. [3,7,10]

H₁(t) = rH₀(t)   ⇒   S₁(t) = S₀(t)r

The coherent method applies the HR within each subgroup, then averages predicted survival. The shortcut applies it directly to the average control survival. For a common protective HR between zero and one, xʳ is concave: Jensen's inequality means powering the average is at least as large as averaging the powered outcomes. For a harmful common HR above one the inequality reverses. Equality occurs for homogeneous risks or a null effect (also at baseline). This statement assumes the same constant conditional HR in all groups.

If a compatible marginal HR varies over time, integrate the product of the instantaneous HR and reference hazard: S₁(t)=exp[−∫₀ᵗ HRM(u)h₀M(u)du]. Raising survival to the instantaneous HR is not generally correct. A single marginal Cox HR also does not establish marginal PH.

11. Two meanings of no further treatment effect

If the biological subgroup-level benefit ends at a fixed time, hazards within each subgroup become equal after that time. Yet treated survivors can remain a higher-risk mix than control survivors. Setting the population HR to 1 imposes a different assumption. [10]

S₁(t|x) = exp[−λx{r min(t,w) + max(0,t−w)}]

Here w is the waning time. The two approaches below use the same standardised treatment survival up to w. After w, conditional waning sets the subgroup HR to 1. Marginal waning instead forces population hazards equal, so S₁(t)=S₁(w)S₀(t)/S₀(w). This isolates the difference in the waning assumption rather than introducing an earlier shortcut error.

Jennings et al. (2024) reported that forcing a marginal HR of 1 when the underlying conditional effect waned inflated incremental restricted mean survival by up to 0.8 years, or 57%, in their simulation scenarios. This is not a universal bias factor. Specify whether waning is conditional or marginal and model plausible structural alternatives if only aggregate data are available.

12. From survival to LYs, QALYs and ICERs

Life-years over a finite horizon are the area under OS, also called restricted mean survival time (RMST). Quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) weight life-years by health utility. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) divides incremental costs by incremental QALYs.

LYa = ∫₀ᵀ Sa(t)dt   |   ΔQ = u∫₀ᵀ [S₁(t)−S₀(t)]/(1+d)t dt

In the default 10-year illustration, applying the conditional HR directly to marginal survival overestimates incremental life-years by about 30.4%. With constant utility and fixed incremental cost, the ICER becomes artificially more favourable. In a real model costs also vary with survival, so the direction of ICER bias is not universal.

In partitioned survival models, PFS determines progression-free occupancy; OS−PFS determines progressed-alive occupancy; 1−OS determines dead occupancy. Independently transformed curves can even cross. In state-transition models, changing survivor composition can induce time dependence; competing exit probabilities must be coherent. Do not equate time on treatment with survival without evidence. RMST differences are collapsible under shared population weights, but extrapolation and censoring still matter.

13. Using a new HR with an old drug survival curve

Suppose an older trial supplies KM survival for drug D versus chemotherapy. A newer study reports an HR for D versus atezolizumab but supplies no survival curve. Can the old drug D curve generate atezolizumab survival? This is a methodological illustration, not evidence about any specific indication.

If HR(D versus atezolizumab)=0.70, the required HR for atezolizumab versus D is 1/0.70. If D survival is 0.60, the implied comparator survival is about 0.482 under compatible marginal PH. Verify direction, endpoint, treatment strategy, estimand and population transfer. Compare eligibility, treatment line, biomarkers, subsequent therapies, baseline severity, calendar time and standard of care. Immunotherapy may show delayed separation, crossing or long-term patterns; do not assume any particular pattern without indication-specific evidence.

Similar median OS for drug D across studies supports but does not establish transferability. Two curves can have the same median and entirely different early survival, tail and area. Obtain multiple landmarks, at-risk counts, curve shape, external validation and ideally IPD or subgroup curves. If these are unavailable, document the shortcut as a structural approximation and propagate alternative assumptions to the ICER.

14. Choose the estimand before choosing the method

Non-collapsibility is not an estimation bias by itself. Bias arises when a valid estimate of one target is used as if it represented another. Confounding, selection, missingness, informative censoring, sparse data and misspecification are separate concerns.

  1. Define the endpoint, treatment strategies, target population, follow-up horizon and decision question.
  2. Decide whether the desired contrast is conditional or marginal. For expected economic outcomes, marginal predictions are needed.
  3. Check whether the measure is collapsible under the relevant assumptions. RD, RR, mean difference and RMST difference are collapsible; OR and HR generally are not. Rate ratios and rate differences based on person-time can be non-collapsible. [11]
  4. Assess confounding separately; do not use coefficient changes alone.
  5. Check population overlap, prognostic factors, effect modifiers and compatibility of HR and reference survival. Conditional PH is not evidence of marginal PH.
  6. Prefer predictions averaged over a target population. If IPD is unavailable, use compatible aggregate evidence, subgroup models or a justified approximation with structural sensitivity analysis.
  7. Propagate joint baseline, coefficient and target-population uncertainty through probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), maintaining correlations. No universal HR correction exists.

Suggested reporting: The relative effect was conditional on baseline covariates, whereas the reference KM curve represented marginal survival. Direct application was treated as an approximation rather than an exact PH transformation. Its economic impact was examined in scenario analyses.
